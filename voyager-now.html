Launched 1977 · still talking · one light-day out

Voyager Now

Both Voyagers are still sending data home, nearly half a century after launch, from beyond the bubble the Sun’s wind blows around the planets. This page works out where they are as you read it, from their speed and the day each crossed into interstellar space. Around 18 November 2026, Voyager 1 passes one light-day from Earth: a radio message sent today arrives tomorrow.

In simple words

Voyager 1 and 2, launched in 1977, are the farthest objects people have ever made. This shows where they are right now.

One light-day

Voyager 1 moves away at a steady 3.6 AU a year, but Earth swings around the Sun each year, sometimes towards it and sometimes away. So the distance from Earth wobbles by up to two AU a year on top of the steady climb, and Voyager crosses the one-light-day line, slips back under it, and crosses again.

Running down

Each Voyager carries three plutonium-238 generators that gave 470 W at launch. Plutonium-238 halves every 87.7 years, and the thermocouples that turn its heat into electricity also wear out, so the electric power falls faster than the curve. To keep going, NASA switches instruments off one by one.

For experts: What the model leaves out

Each Voyager is assumed to coast in a straight line at the speed published when it crossed into interstellar space; the Sun’s pull slows it by only a few metres per second each year by now. Voyager 1’s direction comes from its published position in the sky. Voyager 2’s direction is not in our sources, so its distance from Earth is given from the Sun, and is uncertain by up to 1 AU each way. Earth’s position comes from JPL’s approximate planetary elements.

Quiz: five questions

Answer each one by using the lab above, then check. Good for a class: pairs work through them, the teacher reveals the answers.

  1. How many AU is one light-day?

    Use the lab: Look at the dashed red line on the One light-day chart and read its label.

    Show answer

    173.14 AU. Light travels about 173 times the Earth–Sun distance in one day.

  2. When Voyager 1 is exactly one light-day away, how long after you send a message could its reply reach you? Does the page’s now answer match?

    Use the lab: Press Send Voyager 1 a message now and compare the two times it shows: when the message arrives and when the reply could reach you.

    Show answer

    2 days: one day for the message to get there and one day for the reply to come back (signal time = distance ÷ speed of light). The page uses Voyager’s distance today, so its answer is close to 2 days around late 2026 and grows by about 3.6 AU of travel (about half an hour more each way) every year.

  3. How long would Voyager 2 take to cover the distance to the star Ross 248, and why is the published figure much shorter?

    Use the lab: Read the last line of the To the stars box.

    Show answer

    At 15.341 km/s, covering Ross 248's present 10.3 light-years takes about 201,000 years. The published figure is about 42,000 years because the star is moving towards us.

  4. Voyager 1 flies steadily away from the Sun. So why does its distance from Earth cross the one-light-day line, slip back under it, and cross again?

    Use the lab: Study the gold curve on the One light-day chart and read the note and the message below it.

    Show answer

    Voyager moves away at a steady 3.6 AU a year, but Earth circles the Sun, sometimes towards Voyager and sometimes away. That adds a wobble of up to 2 AU a year, so the distance rises in waves and crosses the line more than once.

  5. HistoryBoth Voyagers were launched in 1977. When and at what distance from the Sun did each one cross into interstellar space?

    Use the lab: Read the footer at the bottom of the page (the sources list).

    Show answer

    Voyager 1: 25 August 2012 at 121 AU. Voyager 2: 5 November 2018 at 119.7 AU. Voyager 1 crossed about six years earlier, at nearly the same distance.
