Know Yourself

Psychologists describe personality with five broad traits, the “Big Five”, found again and again in studies around the world. These fifty statements come from the International Personality Item Pool, a free collection built by researchers so that anyone can use good questions. Rate how accurately each one describes you as you generally are now, not as you wish to be. It takes about ten minutes.

Unlike a horoscope, these results come only from your own answers, and the same answers always give the same result. Nothing you choose is sent anywhere or saved.

Your five traits

Each trait is the total of ten answers, from 10 to 50. The gold line marks the middle of the scale (30). This short form has no official national averages, so the scores show where you put yourself, not a percentile ranking.

How to read this honestly

No trait is good or bad. Each has strengths at both ends: a quiet person listens well, a talkative one connects quickly. Personality also changes slowly through life, and your mood today can shift a few answers. Take the test again in a few months and compare.

If answering these made you notice that you have been feeling low, anxious or taken advantage of, it is worth talking to someone. In India, Tele-MANAS is free and open around the clock on 14416 (or 1-800-891-4416), in many Indian languages.

For experts: What the page leaves out

This is not a clinical or diagnostic test. It cannot tell you whether you have a mental-health condition, and it is not meant for hiring, school admission or any decision about a person.

Your score is compared only with the middle of the scale, not with other people: there are no Indian or international norms behind it, so “high” means high on the scale, not higher than most people. The fifty statements are in English and have not been checked here for translation or cultural fit.

A short self-report also cannot catch narrower facets (for example anxiety versus anger within Emotional Stability), how others see you, or how your answers would shift on a different day.

    They describe the opposite end of a trait, so agreeing with them lowers that trait’s score. Mixing both directions stops people from just agreeing with everything.
