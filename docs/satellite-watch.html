Satellite Watch

Where the space stations, Hubble, India’s NavIC navigation satellites and the 150 or so brightest satellites are right now, and when they pass over you. Every position is computed here, in your browser, from the public orbit catalogue that CelesTrak publishes for free from the US Space Force’s tracking data, using the same SGP4 method that catalogue is designed for.

In simple words

See where the space stations, the Hubble telescope and the brightest satellites are right now, and when they pass over you.

Try this first: Press Use my location, then pick Space stations to find a pass you can see with your own eyes.

  • The International Space Station goes round Earth about every 90 minutes.
  • You can see it as a bright, steady, moving star just after sunset or before sunrise, while it is still lit by the Sun.
  • The orbit data refreshes every day from public tracking data.

Tap a dot to follow it. The shaded half of the map is in night. The ring around the chosen satellite is where it is above the horizon; the line is its path over the next and last 90 minutes.

When can I see it?

Your location stays in this page; it is never sent anywhere.

A pass is visible when the satellite is in sunlight while your sky is dark: just after sunset or before sunrise. Times are in your device’s time zone.

Your sky now: centre is straight up, edge is the horizon, north at top.

Then and now

From one beeping ball to thousands of satellites a night. Tap a year to open it; green dots mark India’s part.

  1. 1957Sputnik, object number 2

    On 4 October 1957 the Soviet Union put Sputnik into orbit. People around the world tuned radios to hear it beep and went outside to spot it. In the official list of objects in space, Sputnik’s rocket is number 1 and Sputnik itself is number 2.

  2. 1960sNORAD and the two-line elements

    The American military began keeping a catalogue of everything in orbit, a job later done by NORAD and today by US Space Command. In the early 1960s, mathematician Max Lane worked out ways to predict where a satellite will be from just a few numbers. Those numbers are sent as two lines of text, first designed to fit on punched cards: the “two-line elements” this page uses.

  3. 1975Aryabhata, India’s first satelliteIndia

    On 19 April 1975 a Soviet rocket launched Aryabhata, designed and built in India. It got its own number in the same catalogue and orbited about 600 km up.

  4. 1985CelesTrak starts sharing orbits

    In 1985 T.S. Kelso set up a computer bulletin board about satellites, at first typing orbit numbers in by hand. It grew into CelesTrak, the free website that this page’s data comes from.

  5. 1997–2019Iridium flares come and go

    From 1997, the first Iridium phone satellites had flat, shiny antennas that could catch the Sun and send a flash to the ground, sometimes bright enough to see in daylight. Skywatchers could predict them in advance. The newer Iridium satellites do not flash, and the last of the old ones was brought down by 27 December 2019.

  6. 1998The first piece of the space station

    On 20 November 1998, exactly 28 years before today, a Russian Proton rocket launched Zarya, the first module of the International Space Station. You can follow it on the map at the top of this page.

  7. 2019Starlink trains

    On 23 May 2019 SpaceX launched 60 Starlink internet satellites at once. Soon after launch they crossed the sky in a line, like a string of pearls. Astronomers worried that thousands of bright satellites would leave streaks across their telescope pictures and interfere with radio astronomy, and SpaceX said it would look for ways to make them less shiny.

  8. 2020ISRO’s NETRA watches the skyIndia

    On 14 December 2020 ISRO opened its space situational awareness control centre, NETRA, in Bengaluru. Its job is to keep track of space junk and warn when something might hit an Indian satellite.

  9. 2025Digantara: tracking satellites from spaceIndia

    On 14 January 2025 the Bengaluru company Digantara launched SCOT, a small satellite with a camera that watches other objects in orbit. It is meant to spot objects as small as about 5 cm.

  10. 2026How many are up there now?

    The European Space Agency counts about 15,800 working satellites around Earth (its figures of 17 November 2026), out of about 19,770 satellites still in space.

For experts: Checks

How fresh is this?

Orbits drift as air drag and small engine burns change them, so a snapshot slowly goes stale: low satellites like the space station by a few kilometres a day, which shifts pass times by seconds at first and by minutes after a couple of weeks. High satellites like NavIC stay accurate for months. CelesTrak does not let other websites read its catalogue straight from a browser, so this page carries a snapshot. Refreshing it is a one-line request. The positions are good enough for spotting satellites by eye, not for navigation or collision avoidance.

Quiz: five questions

Answer each one by using the lab above, then check. Good for a class: pairs work through them, the teacher reveals the answers.

  1. How long does the International Space Station take to go once around the Earth?

    Use the lab: Click the ISS dot on the map (or type ISS in Find a satellite) and read Once round the Earth. Check it against the Checks table.

    Show answer

    About 93 minutes (the lab shows about 92.9 min). The ISS orbits only about 400 km up, so it goes round fast.

  2. Compare the ISS with the NavIC satellite NVS-01. How long does each take to go once around, and what does that mean for someone in India?

    Use the lab: Click NavIC under Show and select NVS-01 (IRNSS-1J). Read Once round the Earth and the pass table in When can I see it? Then compare with the ISS.

    Show answer

    ISS: about 93 minutes. NVS-01: about 24 hours (23.94 h). NVS-01 goes round once a day, so from India it stays at almost the same spot in the sky, over the Indian Ocean at about 129.5°E.

  3. Can you see Hubble from London? Compare with Delhi.

    Use the lab: Type HST in Find a satellite. In When can I see it? set Your place to London, then to Delhi, and look at the pass table each time.

    Show answer

    From London: no passes higher than 10°. From Delhi it does pass. Hubble's orbit tilt is only about 28.5°, so it is never seen from far north or far south of the tropics.

  4. Why can you see the space station only just after sunset or just before sunrise?

    Use the lab: Read the note under the pass table in When can I see it? and the In sunlight now line for the ISS.

    Show answer

    A pass is visible only when the satellite is in sunlight while your sky is dark. That happens just after sunset or before sunrise. In daytime the sky is too bright; late at night the station is in Earth's shadow.

  5. HistoryWhat went wrong with India's NVS-02 satellite in 2025, and what orbit is it stuck in?

    Use the lab: Select NVS-02 (IRNSS-1K) and read the note under its details. Then find the NVS-02 row in the Checks table.

    Show answer

    Its engine valve failed in 2025, so it never reached its circular orbit. It is stuck in a stretched orbit between about 210 km and 37,245 km high.
