Mission Shakti · Iridium–Kosmos · the slow pull of the air

Space Debris Lab

About 40,000 objects are tracked circling Earth, and only about 11,000 of them are working satellites. The rest is junk: dead satellites, spent rocket stages, and the fragments of explosions and collisions, all moving at around 7 km/s. The only thing that cleans orbit is the very thin top of the atmosphere, which drags everything down slowly. How slowly depends almost entirely on height. India chose 283 km for its 2019 anti-satellite test so the debris would fall quickly. The 2009 collision of two satellites happened at 789 km, where it does not.

In simple words

When a satellite breaks apart, its pieces stay in orbit. How long they stay depends on how high they are.

Try this first: Press Mission Shakti, 2019, then Break it and watch.

  • Low down, a thin trace of air slowly drags pieces down until they burn up.
  • Higher up, the air is far thinner, and junk can stay for decades or centuries.
  • Space junk moves so fast that even a small piece can badly damage a spacecraft.

How long things stay up

Lifetime for something in a circular orbit, against its height. A fragment is light for its size and falls fast; a satellite is heavier for its size and lasts longer. Each step up in height buys far more time, because the air thins out so quickly.

For experts: What the model leaves out

Air density follows the US Standard Atmosphere 1976, which matches roughly average solar activity. When the Sun is active the upper air swells and debris falls several times faster; when it is quiet, slower. Fragments are thrown in random directions with speeds spread around a typical value, with a long tail of a few much faster pieces, as hypervelocity impacts produce, and each is given a random ratio of mass to drag area between 5 and 60 kg/m². Orbits are averaged over each lap rather than flown point by point, and Earth’s bulge and the Moon are ignored. Real breakups also make many pieces too small to track, which stay up just as long and are just as dangerous.

Quiz: five questions

Answer each one by using the lab above, then check. Good for a class: pairs work through them, the teacher reveals the answers.

  1. About how many objects are tracked circling Earth, and how many of them are working satellites?

    Use the lab: Read the introduction at the top of the page. You can also check the last points on the Counting the junk chart.

    Show answer

    About 40,000 tracked objects (40,230 in 2025), but only about 11,000 working satellites. The rest is junk: dead satellites, spent rocket stages and fragments.

  2. Compare the two real breakups. How long until half the fragments have fallen back?

    Use the lab: In Break something, click Mission Shakti, 2019 and read Half have fallen by. Then click Iridium–Kosmos, 2009 and read it again.

    Show answer

    Mission Shakti (283 km): about 5 days. Iridium–Kosmos (789 km): about 59 years. The page calls the first “Self-cleaning” and the second “Here to stay”.

  3. Start from Mission Shakti and raise only the height to 500 km. What happens to the debris?

    Use the lab: Click Mission Shakti, 2019. Move Height of the breakup to 500 km, keep the other sliders the same, and click Break it and watch.

    Show answer

    Half have fallen only after about 14 months (instead of 5 days), and the last piece takes about 78 years. A little more height buys a lot more time, because the air thins out so quickly with height.

  4. The model says the last Mission Shakti piece falls after 17 months. The real last piece re-entered in 2022, about three years later. Why the difference?

    Use the lab: Read the second row of the Checks table, and the note in What the model leaves out.

    Show answer

    2019–2020 was a quiet-Sun minimum. When the Sun is quiet, the upper air is thinner than this model’s average, so there is less drag and the real pieces stayed up longer.

  5. HistoryWhat happened in space in 2009 at 789 km, and how much debris did it leave?

    Use the lab: Read the introduction, click Iridium–Kosmos, 2009, and read the footer’s source notes and the third row of Checks.

    Show answer

    Two satellites, Iridium 33 and Kosmos 2251, collided at 789 km at 11.7 km/s. Over 2,000 fragments were catalogued by July 2011. At that height the air is too thin to clear them quickly.
