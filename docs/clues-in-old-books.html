Old records, modern tests

Clues in Old Books

Some questions no modern instrument can answer, because the evidence is a few thousand years old. Astronomers still read Babylonian clay tablets, Chinese court chronicles and Sanskrit verses for it. Here are four case files: what the old text says, the test we can run today, and an honest verdict. The first one is new to this site: we checked an ancient Indian claim against NASA’s planetary tables.

In simple words

Very old writings, like clay tablets from Babylon, Chinese court diaries and Sanskrit verses, still hold clues for scientists today. This page opens four “case files”, tests each old claim with modern science, and gives an honest verdict.

Try this first: Start with Case 1, “Did the planets really gather at the start of the Kali Yuga?” (an age that Indian texts say began in 3102 BC). Read where the planets sat that night, then drag the Days from the Kali Yuga epoch slider away from zero and watch them spread out.

  • An old Indian text says all the planets lined up when the Kali Yuga began. The real sky shows a loose group, not one exact point, so the verdict is “partly true”.
  • Eclipses written down long ago show that the Earth’s spin is slowly getting slower, because the Moon’s tides act like a brake.
  • Chinese astronomers saw a “guest star” in the year 1054. Today the leftover cloud from that exploding star is called the Crab Nebula.
Case 1 · Surya Siddhanta I.57 · 3102 BC

Did the planets really gather at the start of the Kali Yuga?

At the start of the age, by their mean motions, all the planets stand together at the first point of Aries (Mesha), apart from their apsides and nodes.Paraphrase of the Surya Siddhanta (Burgess 1860, I.57 and notes). The Kali Yuga begins at midnight at Ujjain, 18 February 3102 BC.

In 1787 the French astronomer Bailly argued this was a real observation, proof of a very old Indian astronomy; Laplace and later Burgess argued the date was computed backwards from the texts’ own numbers. We can now look. JPL’s planetary elements are rated from 3000 BC; this is a century earlier, close enough for positions to within a degree or two.

True geocentric longitudes from JPL Tables 2a and 2b (Standish and Williams). The text speaks of mean positions; the real sky shows true ones, which differ by a few degrees for most planets. The Moon is left out: 3102 BC is so long ago that the uncertainty in Earth’s rotation (about 21 hours) blurs the Moon by over 10°.

Case 2 · Babylonian and Chinese eclipse records

The Earth’s day is getting longer

A total eclipse of the Sun seen at Babylon in 136 BC, written on a clay astronomical diary.One of the records analysed by F. R. Stephenson and L. V. Morrison to measure the Earth’s slowing spin.

The Moon’s tides act as a brake on the Earth. We cannot see that in a lifetime, but an eclipse is a pinpoint in time and place. Compute where an ancient eclipse should have been seen if the Earth had always turned at today’s rate, and the shadow lands thousands of kilometres from where the scribes saw it. The gap measures the slowing, called ΔT.

Case 3 · Song dynasty court records · 1054

A guest star becomes the Crab Nebula

In 1054 Chinese astronomers recorded a “guest star” near Tianguan (ζ Tauri), bright enough to be seen in daylight for weeks.Song dynasty records, as identified with the Crab Nebula by modern astronomers.

Today a cloud of gas sits at that spot, expanding at about 1,500 km/s, with a pulsar spinning 30 times a second at its heart. Measure how fast the filaments fly apart, run the film backwards, and it should point to the explosion.

Case 4 · Vedanga Jyotisha

Dating a text by where the solstice sat

The Vedanga Jyotisha, the oldest Indian astronomy text, puts the winter solstice at the beginning of Shravishtha (Dhanishtha).Lagadha, Vedanga Jyotisha; the date of the text is debated.

The sky slowly turns against the solstices, about one degree every 72 years (precession). If we know which star marked “the beginning of Dhanishtha”, we know when the solstice sat there. The catch is that one word: where exactly the author meant.

Open leads scientists still mine from old records

Solar superstorms

A sudden jump in carbon-14 in tree rings in 774–775 CE (Miyake 2012) points to an extreme burst of particles. Chronicles of red skies and auroras are searched to pin down what happened and how often it could recur.

More lost supernovae

Guest stars in 185, 1006, 1181 and 1572 are matched to remnants in the sky; each match fixes an age for a remnant that physics alone cannot.

Halley’s returns

Chinese and Babylonian sightings going back to 240 BC test models of how planets nudge a comet’s orbit over millennia.

Earth’s rotation

Every newly read eclipse record tightens ΔT, which also carries signals from melting ice and the Earth’s core.

Indian parameter archaeology

The siddhantas’ planet counts are tuned to fit the sky at some epoch. Working out which epoch each set fits best can date when the observations behind them were made.

Quiz: five questions

Answer each one by using the lab above, then check. Good for a class: pairs work through them, the teacher reveals the answers.

  1. On the night the Kali Yuga began, how many of the six bodies sat within 10° of the zero point, and what arc held all six?

    Use the lab: In Case 1, keep Days from the Kali Yuga epoch at 0 and Zero at Revati (ζ Piscium) selected. Read the two summary tiles.

    Show answer

    4 of 6 sit within 10°, and all six fit in a 40.7° arc. Mercury (−21.3°) and Saturn (−32.7°) trail behind, so it was a loose gathering, not one point.

  2. What changes if you measure from a different zero point?

    Use the lab: In Case 1, click Zero opposite Spica (Lahiri) and read the planet list and both summary tiles.

    Show answer

    Every planet shifts by about 4° (the Sun goes from −5.7° to −9.7°). Now only 3 of 6 are within 10°. The arc holding all six stays 40.7°, because moving the zero point does not move the planets against each other.

  3. How much does the date of the Vedanga Jyotisha change if the author meant a point 10° away from the star α Delphini?

    Use the lab: In Case 4, move Where in Dhanishtha the author meant to +10.0°, then to −10.0°, and read Winter solstice at that point in.

    Show answer

    At the star: 1393 BCE. At +10°: 2109 BCE. At −10°: 677 BCE. The solstice drifts about 1° every 72 years, so 10° moves the date by about 720 years.

  4. The Crab Nebula's gas, run backwards, points to about 1091 CE, not 1054. Why the gap?

    Use the lab: Read the verdict under Case 3 · A guest star becomes the Crab Nebula.

    Show answer

    The filaments are not coasting. The pulsar's wind has been pushing them faster ever since, so a straight-line rewind comes up short, about 40 years after 1054. The 1054 Chinese record fixes the true age.

  5. HistoryOld eclipse records show the Earth's spin is slowing. How much shorter was a day in Aryabhata's time than in 1820?

    Use the lab: In Case 2, read the result for 136 BCE first. Then set Year to 499 CE and read The day was shorter than in 1820 by.

    Show answer

    For the Babylonian eclipse of 136 BCE, a steady-Earth clock is off by 3.34 hours and the track lands 4,706 km west. In 499 CE the day was about 26.8 ms (about 27 ms) shorter than in 1820. That is real, but far too small for a naked-eye astronomer or a water clock to notice.
