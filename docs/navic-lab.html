India’s own GPS · geometry of a position fix · four satellites or nothing

NavIC Lab

NavIC is India’s satellite navigation system, built so that India does not depend on America’s GPS. Its satellites hang high over the Indian Ocean: three stay over fixed points, and four trace figure-eights across the equator. A receiver works out where it is by timing signals from several satellites at once. That needs at least four working satellites in view: one each for east, north and height, and one more for the receiver’s own clock. In March 2026, after a string of atomic-clock failures, only three NavIC satellites could still give positions. This page shows what that means.

What a receiver in each city gets

Position error is roughly the error in each satellite’s range times a number called PDOP, which depends only on the satellites’ geometry. Satellites spread across the sky give a small PDOP; bunched satellites give a large one. Below 4 is good; above 10 is poor. Here are the best and worst values across a whole day.

What the model leaves out

Orbits are perfect circles at geosynchronous height, using the published slots: geostationary at 32.5°E, 83°E and 129.5°E, inclined about 5°, and two inclined planes crossing the equator at 55°E and 111.75°E at 29°. The phasing of the two satellites sharing each inclined plane is not published in our sources, so it is assumed to be a quarter of a day apart. (Half a day would put both at the figure-eight’s crossing point at the same moment, twice a day, collapsing the geometry.) When each slot starts its daily loop is a guess too, so the exact time of the worst moment may differ. Signals are counted from 5° above the horizon, and buildings, terrain and the ionosphere are ignored. The map shows geometry only; it is not a forecast of service. NavIC can also use GPS satellites alongside its own in combined receivers, and this page leaves that out.
