Space stations · 1971 to now

Life on the ISS

Since 2 November 2000 there have always been people living in space, on the International Space Station (ISS). It flies about 420 km above us, so fast that the crew sees a sunrise roughly every hour and a half. This page lets you live through one day aboard, then works out the numbers behind it: how fast the station goes, why it slowly sinks, how the crew drinks recycled water, what weightlessness and space radiation do to the body, and where space stations go next, including India's own.

In simple words

Astronauts have lived on the International Space Station without a break since November 2000. This page lets you live through one day aboard, and works out how fast the station flies, why it slowly sinks, and how the crew stays healthy.

Try this first: Under A day aboard, press Play the day and count the sunrises. Then press Exercise time, and in Air, water and food press No recycling, then Today: 98%.

  • The station flies about 420 km up at about 7.7 km every second, so it goes round Earth in about 93 minutes and the crew sees about 16 sunrises a day.
  • A tiny bit of air up there slowly drags it down, so every few weeks a cargo ship fires its engines to push it back up.
  • The crew recycles 98% of its water, and exercises about 2½ hours a day so bones and muscles don't waste away.

Why 16 sunrises a day

Nothing pushes the station forward. It is falling all the time, but it moves sideways so fast that the ground curves away beneath it as fast as it falls. That is an orbit. The lower the orbit, the faster it must go. Gravity sets the speed: speed = √(GM ÷ r), where GM is Earth's gravity number and r is the distance from Earth's centre. Move the sliders.

Falling slowly: drag and reboosts

At 420 km there is still a tiny wisp of air, hundreds of billions of times thinner than the air you breathe. Hitting it at nearly 8 km/s slows the station a little every second, so it sinks. When the Sun is stormy, it heats and puffs up the upper air, and the station sinks faster. Every few weeks a docked cargo ship fires its engines to push it back up: a reboost. Pick the Sun's mood (the height comes from the slider above).

Model: drag = ½ × air density × speed² × 2.2 × 1,500 m² of area, on a 420-tonne station. The air density for each Sun setting is a rough value for that height; real density changes by several times from day to day.

Air, water and food

Water is heavy and costs a lot to launch, so the ISS recycles it: sweat and breath moisture from the air, and urine. A machine added in 2021, the Brine Processor, squeezes the last water out of the leftover salty urine. In June 2023 NASA reported it had lifted recovery from about 93.5% to 98%. Choose a crew and a mission length.

Per person per day: about 2.5 litres of drinking water (NASA's minimum allowance), about 1.8 kg of food including packaging (about 1.5 kg of actual food), and about 0.8 kg of oxygen to breathe. Washing and making oxygen use more water than this; see "What the page leaves out".

What space does to your body

In weightlessness your bones and muscles stop carrying your weight, and the body starts taking them apart. Astronauts can lose up to 1 to 1.5% of their bone each month, faster than an older person on Earth loses in a whole year. That is why each astronaut exercises about 2½ hours a day: on a treadmill (strapped down with bungee cords), a bike, and a weight machine that uses vacuum cylinders instead of weights. Above the air, they also get far more radiation from space. Pick a stay.

Bone: weight-bearing bones such as hips and spine, with too little exercise. Radiation: 0.2 to 0.5 millisieverts (mSv) a day inside the ISS, against about 2.4 mSv a year from nature for the average person on Earth.

    For experts: What the page leaves out

    The orbit is treated as a perfect circle around a round Earth; the real ISS orbit is slightly oval, tilted 51.6° to the equator, and Earth's bulge slowly turns it, which changes the Sun angle ("beta") over weeks. The shadow is drawn as a sharp-edged cylinder; real sunsets fade through a few seconds of orange twilight. Air density at 420 km really changes by many times with the Sun's 11-year cycle and with solar storms, and the station's drag area changes as its solar panels turn, so the drag numbers are only a rough guide. Reboosts are also used to dodge space junk and to line up with arriving spacecraft, not only to undo drag.

    Water: the page counts only drinking water. The crew also uses water for food, washing and flushing, and the station splits water to make oxygen, so the real water budget is larger, and some water arrives inside food. Body: bone and muscle loss differ a lot between people and between bones, and today's exercise machines cut the loss a great deal; a 2022 study found that half a year in space still cost some astronauts bone equal to about 20 years of ageing, and only about half came back a year after landing. The page leaves out eyesight changes, fluid shifting to the head, sleep, mental health and teamwork, which also matter a lot on long missions. The daily timetable is a typical one; real days change with spacewalks, cargo ships and experiments.

    Quiz: five questions

    Answer each one by using the lab above, then check. Good for a class: pairs work through them, the teacher reveals the answers.

    1. How fast does the ISS fly, and how long does it take to go once round the Earth?

      Use the lab: Look at Speed and Once round Earth in Why 16 sunrises a day, with the height slider at 420 km.

      Show answer

      About 7.66 km every second (about 27,600 km/h) and about 92.8 minutes per lap. Lower orbits need to go faster; higher ones go slower.

    2. Why does the crew see about 16 sunrises a day, and can there be a day with no sunset at all?

      Use the lab: Press Play the day in A day aboard and watch the bottom band. Then move the Sun angle to the orbit slider to 75°.

      Show answer

      The station goes round Earth about 15½ times a day and passes through Earth's shadow once each lap, so it gets a sunrise roughly every 93 minutes. When the Sun angle is above about 70°, the station never enters the shadow and stays in sunshine all day.

    3. How much does the ISS sink each month because of air drag with an average Sun, and what happens when the Sun is stormy?

      Use the lab: In Falling slowly: drag and reboosts, press Average Sun, then Stormy Sun, and read Height lost per month and Reboost every.

      Show answer

      With an average Sun, about 2 km a month (about 70 m a day), so a reboost about every 4 weeks. A stormy Sun puffs up the upper air, so the station sinks about three times faster and needs reboosts much more often.

    4. For 7 people over 180 days, how much new drinking water must be launched with today's 98% recycling, compared with no recycling?

      Use the lab: In Air, water and food, set 7 people and 180 days, then press No recycling and Today: 98%.

      Show answer

      About 3,150 litres with no recycling, but only about 63 litres at 98%. That saves about 3 tonnes of launch weight. Food can't be recycled: about 2,268 kg must still be sent up.

    5. HistoryWho was the first Indian aboard the ISS, and on which mission and year?

      Use the lab: Read Space stations, 1971 to today.

      Show answer

      Shubhanshu Shukla, on Axiom-4. He launched on 25 June 2025, docked on 26 June, and returned on 15 July 2025, 41 years after Rakesh Sharma became the first Indian in space on the Soviet station Salyut 7 in 1984.
