Orbital refilling · Moon or Mars · propellant made on Mars

Starship Mars Campaign

The step after catching rockets: refill a ship in Earth orbit with a fleet of tankers, then send it to the Moon or to Mars, and on Mars make the propellant to fly home. Set the mission below; the mission computer works out the propellant, the tanker flights, the launch windows and the trip home.

In simple words

Plan a trip to Mars or the Moon in a giant Starship. A fleet of tanker rockets fills it up in orbit around Earth, and on Mars it makes its own fuel to fly home.

Try this first: In the Mission planner, keep Mars picked and press Fly this plan. Then click Moon, or drag the Trip time slider, and watch how many tanker flights you need.

  • The ship cannot lift enough fuel from the ground, so tanker rockets fly up one after another to fill it in space. Smaller tankers mean more trips.
  • Rushing costs a lot. On the fastest trip the ship hits Mars too fast for its heat shield, so it must burn extra fuel to slow down and needs many times more fuel.
  • Landing on the Moon takes more rocket power than landing on Mars. Mars has air that does most of the braking, but the Moon has none, so every slowdown is a rocket burn.

Ancient planet clocks

Ancient Indian astronomy still finds this month's Mars window

The Vedas count time with a wheel: Rig Veda 1.164.48 describes one wheel with twelve fellies, three naves and 360 spokes, the ritual year of twelve months, and verse 1.164.11 the twelve-spoked wheel of order carrying 720 children in pairs, the days and nights. The precise planetary numbers come later, from the siddhanta texts. The Surya Siddhanta gives whole revolutions in an age of 4,320,000 years and has every mean planet start together at the beginning of the Kali Yuga, midnight at Ujjain, 18 February 3102 BC. Aryabhata's Aryabhatiya (499 CE) does the same with slightly different counts, and Varahamihira's Pancha Siddhantika (c. 550 CE) gives the older Surya Siddhanta as day-count rules from its own epoch, midnight at Ujjain on 20/21 March 505. Brahmagupta's Brahmasphutasiddhanta (628 CE) works on a far longer cycle: a kalpa of 4,320,000,000 years in which every planet starts and ends at zero.

Run those counts forward 5,128 years and they still place the Earth-Mars launch window that opens this month within days of the modern calculation. Switch the planner's planet clock to try them. More of Aryabhata's ideas, tested on spaceflight.

Quiz: five questions

Answer each one by using the lab above, then check. Good for a class: pairs work through them, the teacher reveals the answers.

  1. With the starting Mars plan, how much propellant must be in the ship's tanks before it leaves Earth orbit, and how many tanker flights fill it?

    Use the lab: Keep Destination: Mars and the starting settings. Read the Propellant table and the summary line above Fly this plan.

    Show answer

    564 t are needed in the tanks, and it takes 6 tanker flights over 42 days.

  2. How does the size of each tanker change the number of tanker flights?

    Use the lab: Move Propellant per tanker to 150 t and read the summary line. Then move it to 50 t.

    Show answer

    At 150 t per tanker it takes 4 flights (28 days). At 50 t it takes 11 flights (77 days). Smaller loads mean more trips to deliver the same propellant.

  3. What does it cost to get to Mars in the shortest trip time?

    Use the lab: Drag Trip time all the way to the fastest setting (112 days) and read the Velocity budget and Propellant tables.

    Show answer

    The Mars entry speed becomes 12.02 km/s, above the 7.5 km/s heat-shield limit, so a 4.52 km/s braking burn is added. The tanks now need about 4,262 t, against 564 t for the 259-day trip.

  4. Why does a Moon landing need a bigger velocity budget than a Mars landing?

    Use the lab: Read the Total from Earth orbit in the Velocity budget for Mars. Then click Moon and read the total again.

    Show answer

    Mars needs 4.34 km/s from Earth orbit; the Moon needs 8.90 km/s. The Moon has no air to brake on, so every slowdown must be a rocket burn. At Mars the atmosphere does most of the braking, leaving only a 0.75 km/s landing burn.

  5. HistoryHow close were the old Indian astronomy texts to modern values for the length of the year and of Mars's year?

    Use the lab: Scroll to Ancient Indian astronomy still finds this month's Mars window and read the rows Sidereal year and Mars year in the table.

    Show answer

    The Surya Siddhanta gives a sidereal year of 365.25876 days and the Aryabhatiya (499 CE) 365.25868; the modern value is 365.25637. All four ancient texts give Mars's year as 687.00 days; the modern value is 686.98. Very close, after more than a thousand years.
