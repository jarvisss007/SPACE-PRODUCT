Mangalyaan Mars Lab

On 24 September 2014 India’s Mars Orbiter Mission fired its engine for 23 minutes and was captured by Mars, making India the first Asian nation to reach Mars orbit. It cost about ₹450 crore (US$73 million), the cheapest Mars mission to date. ISRO published every burn: when, how long, and the orbit that resulted. This page rebuilds the whole voyage from those numbers and checks it against what really happened.

298 days across the Sun

The trip in numbers

The path between Earth and Mars is solved exactly from the two real dates, leaving Earth on 30 November 2013 at 19:19 UTC and arriving at Mars on 24 September 2014 at 02:10 UTC, using JPL’s planet positions. Nothing is fitted.

Leaving Earth one step at a time

Like Chandrayaan-3, Mangalyaan could not be thrown straight at Mars. India’s PSLV rocket could only put it in a stretched Earth orbit, and the spacecraft’s own 440-newton engine raised that orbit in stages, always firing at the low point. Then came the burn that sent it to Mars, and 298 days later the one that braked it into Mars orbit. For each burn the model works out the push from ISRO’s before-and-after orbits, and from that push it predicts how long the engine had to fire.

One number is fitted: the engine’s efficiency (specific impulse). It is chosen so the propellant used across all the burns leaves the 40 kg ISRO reported in the tank after arriving at Mars. It comes out at , a little below the usual figure for this class of engine, because the model leaves out the small course corrections and the losses from firing for many minutes rather than in an instant. Every burn time is then a prediction.

Checks against the real mission

Could they have left on another day?

Earth and Mars line up for a cheap trip only once every 26 months. Each square below is a different pair of departure and arrival dates. Its colour is the total push needed, from Mangalyaan’s parking orbit to its orbit around Mars, with the path for each pair solved the same way. Tap a square to see that trip. The ring marks the dates ISRO chose.

What the model leaves out

Every burn is treated as instantaneous. Long burns lose some push to gravity, which is why the real departure burn ran longer than predicted. The Earth orbit is assumed to keep a 252 km low point after the first burn, the figure ISRO gave then. The three small course corrections on the way (40.5 s, 16 s and 4 s on the small thrusters) are left out. The interplanetary path ignores the planets’ pull while the spacecraft is between them. The Mars arrival is worked out in a plane, while the real orbit was tilted about 150°, so the braking burn is an estimate.

Quiz: five questions

Answer each one by using the lab above, then check. Good for a class: pairs work through them, the teacher reveals the answers.

  1. How long did Mangalyaan’s engine really fire to brake into Mars orbit?

    Use the lab: In Leaving Earth one step at a time, find the Mars orbit insertion row and read the Engine time: real column.

    Show answer

    1,388.67 s, about 23 minutes. The small 440-newton engine needed a long burn to remove about 1,063 m/s.

  2. Burn 4 on 10 November 2013 fell short. How big was its push, and how much did the unplanned extra burn the next day add?

    Use the lab: In the burns table, compare the Push column for Burn 4, fell short and Burn 4b, unplanned extra. Also see the first row of Checks against the real mission.

    Show answer

    Burn 4 gave only 35 m/s (ISRO’s figure; the model gets 32.5 m/s from the orbits). Burn 4b added about 126 m/s to finish the job and raise the far point to 118,642 km.

  3. Did ISRO pick good dates? Compare its trip with the cheapest pair of dates on the launch-window chart.

    Use the lab: In Could they have left on another day?, read the text under the chart. Then tap a few squares far from the ring and watch the total push change.

    Show answer

    ISRO’s dates need about 1.68 km/s in all. The cheapest pair (leave 4 Dec 2013, arrive 22 Sept 2014) needs about 1.67 km/s. So ISRO was almost exactly at the best point. Squares far from the ring need much more push.

  4. For the trans-Mars injection burn, the model predicts a shorter engine time than the real one. Why?

    Use the lab: In the burns table, compare real and predicted engine time for Trans-Mars injection. Then read What the model leaves out.

    Show answer

    Real 1,328.89 s, predicted about 1,219 s (−8%). The model treats every burn as instant. A real burn that lasts many minutes loses some push to gravity, so the engine had to fire longer.

  5. HistoryWhat did India achieve on 24 September 2014, and what did the mission cost?

    Mangalyaan was captured by Mars after a 23-minute burn. India became the first Asian nation to reach Mars orbit. It cost about ₹450 crore (US$73 million), the cheapest Mars mission to date.
