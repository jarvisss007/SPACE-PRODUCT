Hubble · Webb · India’s AstroSat · the wave nature of light

Telescope Lab

No telescope can show a point of light as a perfect point. Light is a wave, and passing through a round mirror spreads each star into a tiny disc with faint rings, the “Airy pattern”. The bigger the mirror, the smaller the disc; the longer the wavelength, the bigger it gets. Two stars closer together than about one disc width merge into one blob. That single rule decides what every telescope can and cannot see.

Why Webb has to be so cold

Everything warm glows. A telescope at room temperature glows most strongly at about 10 µm, right in the middle of the infrared that Webb was built to see, so a warm Webb would be dazzled by itself. Its sunshield keeps the mirror below 50 K, which moves its own glow far out of the way. Drag to see where an object’s glow peaks.

What the model leaves out

The images assume a perfect, unobstructed round mirror. Real telescopes have a secondary mirror and its supports in the way, which add spikes, and Webb’s hexagonal segments give its famous six-pointed stars. Distances to Mars and Pluto are typical close-approach values. AstroSat’s ultraviolet telescope is shown at its 38 cm aperture; detector pixels can limit real images before diffraction does. Peak glow wavelengths come from Wien’s law for an ideal black body.

Quiz: five questions

Answer each one by using the lab above, then check. Good for a class: pairs work through them, the teacher reveals the answers.

  1. How much more light does Webb gather than Hubble?

    Use the lab: Click the Webb button and read Light gathered, compared with Hubble's 4.0 m² under What it can make out.

    Show answer

    25.4 m², about 6.4 times Hubble's 4.0 m². A bigger mirror collects more light, so it can see fainter objects.

  2. Could Hubble see the Apollo lunar module left on the Moon?

    Use the lab: Click Hubble and leave the Colour of light observed slider at its starting value (about 501 nm). Read the Smallest detail for The Moon in the table.

    Show answer

    No. Hubble's smallest detail on the Moon is about 98 m, but the lunar module is only 4 m wide. It is far smaller than one blur disc.

  3. For Hubble, how does the sharpest detail change when you switch from violet light to red light?

    Use the lab: Click Hubble. Move the Colour of light observed slider to about 400 nm (violet) and read Sharpest detail at this colour. Then move it to about 700 nm (red) and read it again.

    Show answer

    About 42 milliarcseconds in violet and about 73 milliarcseconds in red. The blur disc is 1.22 λ/D, so a longer wavelength gives a bigger disc and a less sharp image.

  4. Why must Webb be kept colder than 50 K?

    Use the lab: In Why Webb has to be so cold, set Temperature of the glowing object to 300 K and read where the glow peaks. Then set it to 50 K.

    Show answer

    At 300 K the glow peaks at about 9.7 µm, inside Webb's 0.6–28.5 µm range, so a warm Webb would be dazzled by itself. At 50 K the peak moves to about 58 µm, out of the way. Peak wavelength = 2,898 ÷ temperature (Wien's law).

  5. HistoryHubble (1990), India's AstroSat (2015) and Webb (2021) were launched years apart. Which has the biggest mirror, and can AstroSat UVIT split the two stars that Hubble splits?

    Use the lab: Click each of Hubble, AstroSat UVIT and Webb and read the Mirror line. Keep both sliders at their starting values and read the message under the star picture for Hubble and for AstroSat UVIT.

    Show answer

    Mirrors: Hubble 2.4 m, AstroSat UVIT 38 cm, Webb 6.5 m, the biggest. Hubble shows Two stars (1.2 disc-widths apart). AstroSat UVIT shows One blob (0.19 disc-widths), because its smaller mirror makes a bigger blur disc.

Sources: Wikipedia articles “Hubble Space Telescope” (2.4 m mirror, 4.0 m² collecting area, ultraviolet to near-infrared, theoretical resolution of about 0.05 arcsec for a 2.5 m optical telescope, ground-based seeing of 0.5–1.0 arcsec), “James Webb Space Telescope” (6.5 m, 25.4 m², 0.6–28.5 µm, about six times Hubble’s collecting area, comparable resolution because it works in the infrared, kept below 50 K) and “AstroSat” (Ultraviolet Imaging Telescope).
