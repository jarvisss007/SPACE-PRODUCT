Indian Astronomy Through Time

People in India have been timing the Sun, Moon and stars for at least three thousand years: first to fix the dates of rituals and festivals, then to predict eclipses, then to measure the sky with giant stone instruments, and today with radio dishes, mountain telescopes and spacecraft. Move through time and watch the map fill up.

In simple words

People in India have studied the sky for about 3,000 years: first with their eyes and maths, then with giant stone instruments, and now with radio dishes and spacecraft. This page puts all of it on one map.

Try this first: Press Play 3,000 years and watch the map of India light up. Then tap any dot to read its story.

  • About 1,500 years ago, Aryabhata worked out that the Earth spins, and measured one turn to within a hundredth of a second, with no telescope.
  • Indian ways of counting, including zero, travelled to Baghdad and from there around the world.
  • The same curiosity runs from old books to today's missions: India now has its own telescopes on mountains and spacecraft at the Moon, Mars and the Sun.

A stone clock you can almost watch move

The Samrat Yantra in Jaipur, finished in 1734, is a giant sundial 27 metres tall. Its curved hour scales sit 15.15 metres from the sloping wall that casts the shadow. Earth turns once a day, so the shadow sweeps 2π × 15.15 m in a day: that is a second. You can see it creep.

Each mark on the scale is two seconds of time, so one mark is about 2 mm. That is why the dial can be read to about two seconds.

For experts: How far to trust it
  • Dates. Every date was checked against at least one reference work (see the sources below). Where scholars disagree, the page says so and shows the range, not one number.
  • The Vedanga Jyotisha. Its description of the winter solstice fits the sky of about 1400–1200 BCE if read literally. Many scholars think the text itself was written much later (after 700 BCE or even in the last centuries BCE), for reasons of language and similarities to Babylonian works. The dot is placed at the early reading and marked "debated"; its place is not known, so its ring is dashed.
  • Nilakantha's model. Historians agree he had planets going round the Sun while the Sun goes round Earth, but not on which planets: some read all five, others only Mercury and Venus.
  • Aryabhata's numbers are as given in the Aryabhatiya and reported in standard histories; the modern values are today's sidereal day (86,164.0905 s) and sidereal year (365.256363 days).
  • The map is a simple land outline with no borders, about 0.1° accurate. Dots are at the town or site; space missions are shown at Sriharikota, where they were launched.
  • The shadow speed is worked out here from the 15.15 m radius given by UNESCO, not copied from a guide book. It is the speed along the middle of the scale.
For experts: What the page leaves out
  • Astronomy in Persian and Arabic in India: the Delhi Sultanate and Mughal courts, Mahendra Suri's book on the astrolabe (1370), and the star tables Jai Singh's astronomers used.
  • Many more scholars and texts: Lalla, Bhaskara I, Manjula, Shripati, Shatananda, Achyuta Pisharati and others, and the regional calendars still in use.
  • Nalanda and other centres of learning: see the Nalanda page.
  • Modern institutes beyond the few shown: TIFR, PRL, IUCAA, ARIES Nainital, the Devasthal telescope, and India's share in the LIGO-India and SKA projects.
  • How old records help science today: see Clues in old books, which also shows how a text is dated from where the solstice sat.

Quiz: five questions

Answer each one by using the lab above, then check. Good for a class: pairs work through them, the teacher reveals the answers.

  1. HistoryHow old was Aryabhata when he wrote the Aryabhatiya, and in what year?

    Use the lab: Press Golden age, 500–1150 and read the first story.

    Show answer

    He says he was 23, and the book is dated to 499 CE.

  2. Aryabhata's day (one turn of the Earth) was 23 h 56 m 4.1 s. How far off was he?

    Use the lab: Look at How close did they get?

    Show answer

    Today's value is 23 h 56 m 4.09 s, so he was off by about one hundredth of a second: precise to about 1 part in 9 million.

  3. HistoryWhich place in Kerala gave its name to a famous school of mathematics and astronomy, and what did Madhava find there?

    Use the lab: Press Kerala school.

    Show answer

    Sangamagrama, near Irinjalakuda. Madhava wrote π and the sine as never-ending sums, about 250 years before Europeans found the same series.

  4. How fast does the shadow move on Jaipur's giant sundial, and how is that worked out?

    Use the lab: Read A stone clock you can almost watch move.

    Show answer

    About 1.1 mm a second. The scale is 15.15 m from the shadow's edge, and in one day the shadow goes once round a circle of that size: 2π × 15.15 m ÷ 86,400 seconds.

  5. Slide the year to 2000. How many space missions are lit, and which recent mission carries on Kodaikanal's work of watching the Sun?

    Use the lab: Move the year slider, then press Space age and step with Later ▸.

    Show answer

    By 2000 only the Aryabhata satellite (1975) is lit. Aditya-L1, at the L1 point since 6 January 2024, watches the Sun like Kodaikanal has since 1899.
