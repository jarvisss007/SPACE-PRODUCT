Real engines · real physics · your rocket

Rocket Lab

Start from a real rocket or design your own: pick engines from the catalogue, stack stages, load propellant, set the payload, and fly it. The simulator flies the ascent with thrust that changes with altitude, air drag, gravity and a gravity turn, then tells you what orbit you reached and how much push is left for the Moon or Mars.

In simple words

Build a rocket from real engines and see how far it can go: to orbit, to the Moon, or to Mars.

Try this first: Pick a ready-made rocket at the top, like Saturn V or LVM3, and press launch. Then swap one engine and see what changes.

  • A rocket is mostly fuel: to go further you need far more fuel, not just a little more.
  • Engines that throw their exhaust out faster save fuel, which is why hydrogen engines are used for the top stages.
  • Rockets drop empty stages on the way up so they carry less dead weight.

Then and now

From gunpowder arrows to rockets that land themselves. Tap a year to open it; green dots mark India’s part.

  1. ~1200sChinese fire arrows

    Soon after gunpowder was invented in China, people packed it into tubes tied to arrows. By about the 1200s these “fire arrows” flew under their own push in battle: the first rockets.

  2. 1780sTipu Sultan’s rockets, then Congreve’sIndia

    The armies of Mysore under Hyder Ali and Tipu Sultan fired rockets with iron cases, which could hold more pressure and fly further than paper or bamboo ones; Tipu’s rocket corps grew to about 5,000 men. After Tipu fell in 1799, captured rockets went to Britain, and William Congreve’s copies were fired at Boulogne in 1806.

  3. 1903Tsiolkovsky’s rocket equation

    Russian schoolteacher Konstantin Tsiolkovsky worked out how much speed a rocket gains from the fuel it burns. It is the rule behind this whole lab: to go a bit faster you need a lot more fuel.

  4. 1926Goddard’s liquid-fuel rocket

    On 16 March 1926, in a Massachusetts field, Robert Goddard launched the first rocket burning liquid fuel and liquid oxygen. It flew for about 2.5 seconds and rose about 12 m, but every big rocket since is its descendant.

  5. 1944The V-2 reaches space

    Germany’s V-2 was a weapon, built partly by forced labour, but in 1944 one became the first human-made object to cross into space. After the war its engineers and rockets helped start both the US and Soviet space programmes.

  6. 1957Sputnik

    On 4 October 1957 a Soviet R-7 rocket put Sputnik, a beeping metal ball, into orbit. The space age had begun.

  7. 1967–73Saturn V

    The giant that sent Apollo astronauts to the Moon first flew in November 1967 and carried Apollo 11 in 1969. It is the first rocket you can fly in this lab.

  8. 1980SLV-3 puts Rohini in orbitIndia

    On 18 July 1980 India’s own SLV-3, led by A. P. J. Abdul Kalam, put the small Rohini satellite into orbit. India became one of only a handful of countries able to launch its own satellites.

  9. 1994PSLV, the workhorseIndia

    The PSLV first reached orbit successfully in October 1994. It went on to launch Chandrayaan-1 (2008), the Mars Orbiter Mission (2013) and a record 104 satellites in one go (2017).

  10. 2014GSLV’s home-made cryogenic stageIndia

    Hydrogen engines need fuel colder than −250 °C and are very hard to build. On 5 January 2014 a GSLV flew with an Indian-built cryogenic upper stage for the first time and reached orbit.

  11. 2015A booster lands itself

    On 21 December 2015 a SpaceX Falcon 9 first stage flew back and landed upright in Florida after launching satellites. Reusing boosters has since made launches much cheaper.

  12. 2023LVM3 and Chandrayaan-3India

    ISRO’s biggest rocket, LVM3 (the other preset in this lab), launched Chandrayaan-3 on 14 July 2023. It landed on 23 August 2023, making India the first country to land near the Moon’s south pole.

  13. 2023–26Starship

    SpaceX’s Starship, the biggest and most powerful rocket ever flown, first launched in April 2023 and is built to be fully reusable. On its 14th test flight, on 28 September 2026, it reached orbit for the first time and released 26 Starlink satellites.

  14. 2026Vikram-1: India’s first private rocket in orbitIndia

    On 18 July 2026 Skyroot Aerospace’s Vikram-1 lifted off from Sriharikota and placed small satellites in an orbit about 450 km up. It was the first time a rocket built by an Indian company, not ISRO, reached orbit.

For experts: How far to trust it

Before you rely on a number from a model, check it against something real. Here are the two rockets whose full stage data is published, flown by this simulator against what they actually did.

The simulator flies one fixed pitch program and simple guidance; real rockets fly trajectories optimised for each mission, which is worth several percent. For LVM3 we also had to assume the S200 booster’s sea-level performance and use its average thrust, and its upper stage pushes with less than half a g, where the steering a real flight computer does matters most. Treat results within about 10% of a goal as a coin toss.

Quiz: five questions

Answer each one by using the lab above, then check. Good for a class: pairs work through them, the teacher reveals the answers.

  1. How heavy is the Saturn V at liftoff, and how much stronger is its thrust than its weight?

    Use the lab: Click Saturn V (Apollo) and read Mass at liftoff and Thrust over weight at liftoff under the flight chart.

    Show answer

    About 2,848 t, with thrust over weight of about 1.21. Thrust must be more than 1 times the weight, or the rocket cannot leave the pad.

  2. Compare Saturn V and ISRO’s LVM3. How much push is left in the top stage once each reaches orbit, and which targets can each reach?

    Use the lab: Click Saturn V (Apollo) and read Push left in the top stage and the four target boxes. Then click ISRO LVM3 and compare.

    Show answer

    Saturn V: about 3.10 km/s, enough for geostationary transfer and only about 0.05 km/s short of the Moon (inside the model’s error). LVM3 with its preset payload: about 1.9 km/s, only enough for low orbit, short of the Moon by about 1.2 km/s.

  3. What is the most LVM3 can lift to low orbit in this simulator? How does that compare with ISRO’s figure?

    Use the lab: Click ISRO LVM3, then click Find the most it can lift to orbit. Compare with the LVM3 row in How far to trust it.

    Show answer

    About 7.0 t, against ISRO’s 10 t, so the model is about 30% pessimistic. It flies one fixed pitch program and has to assume the S200 booster’s sea-level figures, while real flights steer an optimised path.

  4. In a blank design, change stage 1’s engine to the J-2. Why can’t the rocket leave the pad?

    Use the lab: Click A blank design. In Stage 1, set Engine to J-2 (Saturn V upper stages). Read the verdict and the small note under the stage. Check the Engine catalogue too.

    Show answer

    Thrust over weight drops to 0.00 and it “never left the pad”. The J-2 is a vacuum engine: it has no sea-level thrust (shown as — in the catalogue), so it cannot run at the ground. It was built for upper stages that light high up.

  5. HistoryHow much did Saturn V send to the Moon for Apollo, and which Indian Moon mission did LVM3 launch?

    Use the lab: Read the Other rockets, published figures table, rows Saturn V and LVM3 (ISRO), and the first row of How far to trust it.

    Show answer

    Saturn V sent 43.5 t to the Moon. LVM3 launched Chandrayaan-3 and can send about 3 t to the Moon. With Apollo’s 43.5 t, the simulator’s Saturn V is within about 2% of the push the Moon needs.
