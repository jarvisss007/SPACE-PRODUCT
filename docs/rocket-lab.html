Rocket Lab

Start from a real rocket or design your own: pick engines from the catalogue, stack stages, load propellant, set the payload, and fly it. The simulator flies the ascent with thrust that changes with altitude, air drag, gravity and a gravity turn, then tells you what orbit you reached and how much push is left for the Moon or Mars.

How far to trust it

Before you rely on a number from a model, check it against something real. Here are the two rockets whose full stage data is published, flown by this simulator against what they actually did.

The simulator flies one fixed pitch program and simple guidance; real rockets fly trajectories optimised for each mission, which is worth several percent. For LVM3 we also had to assume the S200 booster’s sea-level performance and use its average thrust, and its upper stage pushes with less than half a g, where the steering a real flight computer does matters most. Treat results within about 10% of a goal as a coin toss.

Quiz: five questions

Answer each one by using the lab above, then check. Good for a class: pairs work through them, the teacher reveals the answers.

  1. How heavy is the Saturn V at liftoff, and how much stronger is its thrust than its weight?

    Use the lab: Click Saturn V (Apollo) and read Mass at liftoff and Thrust over weight at liftoff under the flight chart.

    Show answer

    About 2,848 t, with thrust over weight of about 1.21. Thrust must be more than 1 times the weight, or the rocket cannot leave the pad.

  2. Compare Saturn V and ISRO’s LVM3. How much push is left in the top stage once each reaches orbit, and which targets can each reach?

    Use the lab: Click Saturn V (Apollo) and read Push left in the top stage and the four target boxes. Then click ISRO LVM3 and compare.

    Show answer

    Saturn V: about 3.10 km/s, enough for geostationary transfer and only about 0.05 km/s short of the Moon (inside the model’s error). LVM3 with its preset payload: about 1.9 km/s, only enough for low orbit, short of the Moon by about 1.2 km/s.

  3. What is the most LVM3 can lift to low orbit in this simulator? How does that compare with ISRO’s figure?

    Use the lab: Click ISRO LVM3, then click Find the most it can lift to orbit. Compare with the LVM3 row in How far to trust it.

    Show answer

    About 7.0 t, against ISRO’s 10 t, so the model is about 30% pessimistic. It flies one fixed pitch program and has to assume the S200 booster’s sea-level figures, while real flights steer an optimised path.

  4. In a blank design, change stage 1’s engine to the J-2. Why can’t the rocket leave the pad?

    Use the lab: Click A blank design. In Stage 1, set Engine to J-2 (Saturn V upper stages). Read the verdict and the small note under the stage. Check the Engine catalogue too.

    Show answer

    Thrust over weight drops to 0.00 and it “never left the pad”. The J-2 is a vacuum engine: it has no sea-level thrust (shown as — in the catalogue), so it cannot run at the ground. It was built for upper stages that light high up.

  5. HistoryHow much did Saturn V send to the Moon for Apollo, and which Indian Moon mission did LVM3 launch?

    Use the lab: Read the Other rockets, published figures table, rows Saturn V and LVM3 (ISRO), and the first row of How far to trust it.

    Show answer

    Saturn V sent 43.5 t to the Moon. LVM3 launched Chandrayaan-3 and can send about 3 t to the Moon. With Apollo’s 43.5 t, the simulator’s Saturn V is within about 2% of the push the Moon needs.
