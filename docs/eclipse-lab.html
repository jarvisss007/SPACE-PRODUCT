From Rahu to relativity · NASA’s eclipse elements · your city

Eclipse Lab

For 2,500 years people have tried to predict the moment the Moon swallows the Sun. In India the story was Rahu, a severed head chasing the Sun. In 499 CE Aryabhata wrote, in the chapter of his book called the Golapada, the chapter this website is named after, that eclipses are shadows: the Moon covering the Sun, and Earth’s shadow falling on the Moon. Today the same idea, worked out with NASA’s numbers, tells you to the second when the sky will go dark over your city.

In simple words

A solar eclipse happens when the Moon passes in front of the Sun. This page shows when, and how much of the Sun is covered, from your city.

Try this first: Choose an Eclipse and Where you watch, then watch the Moon cross the Sun.

  • Only along a narrow path does the Moon cover the whole Sun: that is a total eclipse. Everywhere else it takes only a bite.
  • Eclipses come in families that repeat every 18 years and 11 days.
  • Never look at the Sun without proper eclipse glasses, except during the few minutes when it is fully covered.

The family of 2027: Saros 136

Every 18 years, 11 days and 8 hours (one saros, 223 lunar months), the Sun, Moon and the Moon’s orbit line up almost exactly as before, and the eclipse repeats. The extra 8 hours mean Earth has turned a third of the way round, so each repeat lands about 120° further west. Babylonian astronomers knew this cycle centuries before the common era. The eclipse of 2 August 2027 is a member of Saros series 136, and so is the 1919 eclipse that proved Einstein right and the 2009 eclipse that darkened India.

    Never look at the Sun without eclipse glasses

    Even when 99% is covered, the thin crescent left can burn your retina without any pain. Use certified solar viewing glasses or a pinhole projector. Only during totality itself, the minutes when the Sun is completely covered, is it safe to look with bare eyes. Put the glasses back on the moment the first bead of sunlight returns. A ring of fire (annular) eclipse is never safe to look at directly.

    Then and now

    More of the story, to add to the history above: clay tablets, the first eclipse map, and India’s eclipses up to the next one. Tap a year to open it; green dots mark India’s part.

    1. ~747 BCEBabylon’s sky diaries

      For about 700 years, from around 747 BCE to 61 BCE, scribes in Babylon pressed notes about the sky into clay tablets, night after night, along with the weather and the price of grain. Several hundred of these tablets survive, most of them in the British Museum. Careful records like these helped lead to the saros cycle.

    2. 136 BCEAn old eclipse that clocks the Earth

      A Babylonian tablet says the Sun was eclipsed in Babylon on 15 April 136 BCE. If Earth had always spun at today’s speed, that eclipse should have been seen far to the west, over the Mediterranean. It was not, because Earth’s spin is slowly braking. Records like this show the day grows about 1.8 thousandths of a second longer every century. This page uses that drift, called ΔT, for every eclipse it draws.

    3. 1715Halley’s eclipse map

      Before the eclipse of 3 May 1715, Edmond Halley, the astronomer of Halley’s Comet, printed a map of England showing in advance where the Moon’s shadow would fall. He was off by only about 4 minutes and about 30 km. The map on this page does the same job, worked out from NASA’s numbers.

    4. 1868Seeing the Sun’s flames without an eclipseIndia

      At Guntur, Pierre Janssen did more than spot helium (see above). The bright lines he saw at the Sun’s edge were so strong that he realised he could see them without an eclipse at all, and then did so. Norman Lockyer found the same trick in England that October. Astronomers no longer had to wait years for an eclipse to study the Sun’s edge.

    5. 1995The Diwali eclipseIndia

      The total eclipse of 24 October 1995 fell on Diwali. Its narrow path crossed Rajasthan, Uttar Pradesh, Jharkhand and West Bengal, but totality was short: at most about a minute, at places like Panskura near Kolkata. The Indian Institute of Astrophysics set up camps from Rajasthan to Diamond Harbour, joined by scientists from other countries, and Doordarshan showed it live.

    6. 2009From Surat to TawangIndia

      On 22 July 2009 the shadow crossed India from west to east at sunrise: Surat, Indore, Bhopal, Varanasi, Patna, Guwahati and on to Tawang in Arunachal Pradesh. Totality lasted over 3 minutes in places like Surat, Varanasi and Patna, and thousands of pilgrims watched from the banks of the Ganges in Varanasi.

    7. 2023–24Aditya-L1 reaches its postIndia

      India’s first mission to study the Sun was launched on a PSLV on 2 September 2023 and settled near the L1 point, about 1.5 million km from Earth, on 6 January 2024. From there the Moon never gets in the way, so its VELC coronagraph, built in Bengaluru, makes its own eclipse every day. By April 2026 ISRO had shared about 27 terabytes of its data with scientists.

    8. 2024North America goes dark

      On 8 April 2024 totality crossed Mexico, the United States and Canada, lasting up to 4 min 28 s near Nazas in Mexico. About 44 million people lived inside the path, and around 50 million saw totality: one of the most-watched eclipses ever.

    9. 2026Spain’s first total eclipse in over a century

      On 12 August 2026 totality crossed Greenland, Iceland and northern Spain, from A Coruña and Bilbao to Valencia and Palma, lasting up to about 2 min 18 s off Iceland. It was mainland Spain’s first total eclipse since 1905 and Iceland’s first since 1954. Spain set up 660 official viewing sites. That night the Perseid meteors peaked in a moonless sky.

    10. 2034India’s next totality: Kashmir and LadakhIndia

      After 2009, the next total eclipse seen from India comes on 20 March 2034. Its path crosses the far north: Srinagar, Anantnag, Baramulla and Leh. Totality lasts about 2 minutes there, late in the afternoon. It is in this page’s list of eclipses, so you can try it above.

    For experts: How far to trust it

    The page works out every time below itself from NASA’s Besselian elements. These are the same numbers published eclipse tables are made from. The table compares the page’s times with times published for each city.

    For experts: What the model leaves out

    Contact times use the standard Besselian method (Explanatory Supplement; Meeus, Elements of Solar Eclipses) for an observer at sea level, with NASA’s value of ΔT, the slow drift of Earth’s clock, for each eclipse. Each eclipse’s elements cover about three hours either side of its middle, enough for the whole eclipse. Mountains along the Moon’s edge, which make totality start or end a second or two early, are left out. So is refraction near the horizon. The map shows the maximum coverage on a 2° grid, so the narrow path of totality appears as a few bright squares around the white centre line. City times are in the city’s own clock time, from the browser’s time-zone database.

    Quiz: five questions

    Answer each one by using the lab above, then check. Good for a class: pairs work through them, the teacher reveals the answers.

    1. In the 2009 eclipse, how long did totality last at Patna, and when did it begin by Indian clocks?

      Use the lab: Pick 22 July 2009 and Patna under Your eclipse, then read the cards.

      Show answer

      Totality lasted 3 min 44 s and began at 06:24:39 IST, with the Sun only about 15° above the horizon. This matches the published table to the second.

    2. Will Delhi see the 2 August 2027 eclipse? How much of the Sun is covered, and at what time?

      Use the lab: Pick 2 August 2027 and Delhi. Read Sun covered and Greatest.

      Show answer

      Only a partial eclipse: about 8% of the Sun covered at about 16:29 IST. Try Mumbai, further west, for a deeper partial eclipse.

    3. Why is a ring of fire (annular) eclipse never safe to look at, while totality briefly is?

      Use the lab: Pick 21 May 2031 and Kochi, slide Moment to the middle, and look at the picture. Then read the warning box.

      Show answer

      In an annular eclipse the Moon is too far away to cover the whole Sun, so a bright ring of sunlight always remains (here about 92% is covered, not 100%). Only in totality is all of the bright surface hidden.

    4. HistoryThe 1919, 1955, 2009 and 2027 eclipses are all members of one family, Saros 136. How far apart are they in time, and which had the longest totality?

      Use the lab: Read the table under The family of 2027: Saros 136.

      Show answer

      Each comes one saros later: 18 years, 11 days and 8 hours. The longest totality is 1955, 7 min 08 s, across Sri Lanka and the Andaman Islands.

    5. HistoryWhat did Pierre Janssen discover from Guntur during the eclipse of 18 August 1868, and how long before anyone found it on Earth?

      Use the lab: Read From Rahu to helium to Aditya-L1.

      Show answer

      A new yellow line in the Sun’s spectrum: the element helium. Chemists isolated it on Earth in 1895, 27 years later.
