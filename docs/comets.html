Comets

A comet is a lump of ice, dust and rock a few kilometres wide. Most of the time it is far from the Sun, frozen and dark. When its long, stretched path brings it close to the Sun, the ice warms up and turns straight into gas. The gas and dust puff out into a huge glowing cloud and a tail that can be longer than the distance from the Earth to the Sun. This page plots seven famous comets on their real paths, shows why the tail grows, and tells how people went from fearing comets to predicting them. For shooting stars made by comet dust, see Meteor Showers; for space rocks that might hit Earth, see Asteroid Watch.

In simple words

A comet is a lump of ice and dust a few kilometres wide. When its long path brings it near the Sun, the ice turns to gas and a glowing tail grows. This page plots seven famous comets on their real paths.

Try this first: In the Orbit player, tap Halley and press Play. Then tap 3I/ATLAS, a comet from another star, and drag the time slider.

  • A comet's tail always points away from the Sun, even when the comet is moving away from the Sun.
  • Comets usually wake up inside about 3 au (three times the Earth–Sun distance). Closer in, the ice turns to gas much faster and the tail grows.
  • In 1705 Edmond Halley predicted his comet would return in 1758. It did, and it will be back again on 28 July 2061.

Why the tail grows

Far from the Sun a comet is a frozen, dark lump, too cold for its ice to change. Closer in, sunlight warms the surface and the ice turns straight into gas (this is called sublimation, like dry ice "smoking"). The gas drags dust off the comet with it. Sunlight pushes the dust back into a curved, yellowish dust tail, and the solar wind sweeps the gas into a straight, bluish ion tail. Both point away from the Sun.

The surprise is how fast things change. Sunlight only gets 9 times weaker between 1 au and 3 au. But water ice turns to gas hundreds of times slower at 3 au, because it is so cold there that most of the sunlight's heat is used just to warm it up. That is why comets "switch on" inside about 3 au. Real comets differ: ice of carbon monoxide or carbon dioxide can wake a comet much farther out. Hale-Bopp was already active when it was found at 7.2 au.

From omens to predictions

For thousands of years people saw comets appear without warning and thought they were signs of trouble. Then astronomers learned that comets follow the same laws as planets, and can be predicted. Green = observed, blue = a space mission, gold = future, dashed gold = less certain.

    For experts: What the page leaves out

    The tugs of the planets (the page's orbits are pure Sun-and-comet ellipses and hyperbolas); the push from gas jets (scientists call these "non-gravitational forces"); the difference between the dust tail and the ion tail directions, which the map draws as one; the comet's brightness as seen from Earth; comets that break apart or fade; the Oort Cloud and Kuiper Belt where comets come from; and the exact time zones of old dates. Dates before 1582 in history lists are usually in the old Julian calendar.

    Quiz: five questions

    Answer each one by using the lab above, then check. Good for a class: pairs work through them, the teacher reveals the answers.

    1. Which way does a comet's tail point, and does that change when the comet is moving away from the Sun?

      Use the lab: In the Orbit player, pick Halley and press Play. Watch the tail before and after the comet passes the Sun.

      Show answer

      The tail always points away from the Sun. After the comet goes round the Sun it travels out tail first, because sunlight and the solar wind push the gas and dust outward, whichever way the comet is moving.

    2. How fast was 3I/ATLAS moving at its closest point to the Sun, and why will it never come back?

      Use the lab: Tap 3I/ATLAS, drag the time slider until it says closest to the Sun, and read Speed. Then read the path shape under the comet's facts.

      Show answer

      About 68 km/s (roughly 246,000 km/h). Its path shape number is e = 6.1, far above 1, so its path is an open curve: it came from another star, passes the Sun once and leaves for ever.

    3. At 3 au from the Sun, sunlight is about 1/9 as strong as at Earth. How much slower does water ice turn to gas there than at 1 au?

      Use the lab: In Why the tail grows, set the slider to 3.00 au and read Ice to gas vs 1 au.

      Show answer

      About 1/590, hundreds of times slower, even though the sunlight is only 9 times weaker. That is why comets usually switch on only inside about 3 au.

    4. HistoryWho first spotted Halley's Comet on its predicted return, and when?

      Use the lab: Read From omens to predictions.

      Show answer

      Johann Palitzsch, a German farmer and amateur astronomer, on 25 December 1758. Halley had predicted the return in 1705 but had died in 1742. The comet passed the Sun on 13 March 1759.

    5. Using only the Sun's pull, when would Halley next come closest to the Sun? How does that compare with JPL's answer?

      Use the lab: Open Checks and read the last row of the table.

      Show answer

      The page's Sun-only calculation says about 11 Feb 2062; JPL, which adds the pull of Jupiter, Saturn and the other planets, says 28 July 2061, about 198 days earlier. The planets' tugs matter over a whole orbit.
