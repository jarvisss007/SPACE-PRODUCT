Starship Catch Simulator

A full flight in about two and a half minutes. Thrust, drag and gravity fly the ascent, so the setup below decides whether the ship makes orbit. You can also fly the booster catch yourself.

In simple words

Watch a whole Starship flight in about two and a half minutes: the launch, the booster splitting off, and a giant tower catching the booster as it comes home. Your settings decide whether the ship makes it to orbit, a steady loop around the Earth.

Try this first: In Mission setup, move the Payload slider to load more cargo. Then press Apply and relaunch and watch whether the ship still reaches orbit.

  • Max-Q is the moment in the climb when the air pushes hardest on the rocket, because it is already going fast while the air is still thick.
  • Fuel the booster saves for its trip home is fuel that cannot push the ship upward, so the ship has less to spare.
  • If a few booster engines fail at liftoff, the rocket climbs with less push and separates later, but it can still reach orbit.

    Quiz: five questions

    Answer each one by using the lab above, then check. Good for a class: pairs work through them, the teacher reveals the answers.

    1. At what time in the flight does Starship hit Max-Q, and what is Max-Q?

      Use the lab: Look at the Flight sequence list and click Max-Q to jump there and read its description.

      Show answer

      At T+01:13. Max-Q is maximum dynamic pressure: speed and air density together give the biggest aerodynamic load of the climb.

    2. What is the heaviest payload that still gets the ship into orbit?

      Use the lab: In Mission setup, move the Payload slider up in 5 t steps and read the Flight computer line each time.

      Show answer

      95 t. At 95 t the ship still reaches orbit with almost no propellant to spare. At 100 t the flight computer says it falls short of orbit by about 106 km/h.

    3. What happens to the launch if 3 of the 33 booster engines fail at liftoff?

      Use the lab: Set Engines out at liftoff to 3 of 33 (keep everything else at the start values) and read the Flight computer line.

      Show answer

      Liftoff thrust-to-weight drops from 1.28 to 1.17 and staging comes later, at T+02:47 instead of T+02:32. The ship still reaches orbit, with 2.4% of its propellant to spare. Fewer engines means less push for the same weight.

    4. Why does keeping more fuel in the booster for the trip home take speed away from the ship?

      Use the lab: Set Booster return reserve to 200 t and read the Flight computer line. Then set it to 400 t and read it again.

      Show answer

      With 200 t the booster cannot get home and ditches about 200 km offshore. With 400 t it is caught, but staging comes earlier (T+02:28, about 5,729 km/h) and the ship has only 2.4% to spare instead of 3.0%. Fuel saved for the return is fuel not used to push the ship upward.

    5. HistoryIn this flight the hot-stage ring is thrown away after separation. What does the page say about later boosters?

      Use the lab: In the Flight sequence, click Hot-stage ring jettison (T+03:17) and read the description.

      Show answer

      The vented ring protected the booster dome during hot staging, and it is dropped to save mass for the landing. The page says later booster designs build it in, so the design changed as Starship developed.
