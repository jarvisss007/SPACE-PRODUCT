In simple words

Aryabhata was an Indian astronomer who wrote his famous book in the year 499. This page takes four of his ideas and tests each one on a real space-travel problem, to see where he still holds up.

Try this first: In the first section, “The Earth turns, the stars stand still”, press View from the boat to see his idea as he described it. Then click a launch site such as Sriharikota or Kourou to see how much free speed the spinning Earth gives a rocket heading east.

  • Aryabhata said the Earth spins while the stars stay still. His time for one spin is only about a hundredth of a second away from today’s atomic clocks.
  • His four-step recipe for pi (the number you get when you divide the distance around a circle by the distance across it) gives 3.1416, and he honestly called it close, not exact.
  • He said the Moon shines with sunlight and that eclipses are shadows, not a demon swallowing the Sun or Moon. He was right.

Aryabhata Lab

आर्यभटीय

At 23, Aryabhata wrote the Aryabhatiya, 121 verses in four chapters: numbers, mathematics, time and the sphere. He says when, himself: when three quarter-ages and sixty times sixty years of the Kali Yuga had passed, 23 years of his life had passed. That is 499 CE, so he was born in 476.

This page takes four of his ideas, implements them exactly as he wrote them, and puts each one to work on a modern spaceflight problem. Each test ends with a verdict: where his idea still holds, and where a spacecraft needs more.

Circumference / diameter3.1416

His planet clock is also one of the five in the Mars campaign planner. He is often said to have headed nearby Nalanda; his own book names only Kusumapura, and the sources do not show the Nalanda link.

Idea 1 · Golapada 9 and Dasagitika 1

The Earth turns, the stars stand still

“As a man in a boat going forward sees a stationary object moving backward”Golapada 9, Clark 1930. The verse goes on: so at Lanka the stars appear to drift west.

Most astronomers of his time, and for a thousand years after, said the sky turns around a fixed Earth. Aryabhata said the opposite and counted it: the Earth turns eastward 1,582,237,500 times in a yuga of 4,320,000 years. Subtract the Sun's 4,320,000 trips round the sky and you get the solar days, and from those the length of one turn against the stars.

Spaceflight test. A rocket launched east starts with the ground's speed for free. Using his rotation period, how much speed does each launch site hand a rocket?

Idea 2 · Ganitapada 10

π from four steps of arithmetic

“Add 4 to 100, multiply by 8, and add 62,000.”Ganitapada 10, Clark 1930: the result is approximately the circumference of a circle 20,000 across.

(100 + 4) × 8 + 62,000 = 62,832, and 62,832 ÷ 20,000 = 3.1416. He called it approximate, which it is: it is off by 7 parts in 10 million.

Spaceflight test. Swap his π into every orbit formula a mission planner uses.

Idea 3 · Dasagitika 12

A sine table in 24 numbers

Aryabhata compressed trigonometry into one verse of 24 differences: 225, 224, 222, 219, 215, 210, 205, 199, 191, 183, 174, 164, 154, 143, 131, 119, 106, 93, 79, 65, 51, 37, 22, 7. Add them up in steps of 3¾° and you get the half-chord (jya) of each angle, on a circle of radius 3,438, chosen so that the radius is close to one radian measured in arc-minutes (21,600 ÷ 2π). They sum to exactly 3,438. The word jya, through Arabic jiba and a Latin mistranslation, became our “sine”.

Spaceflight test. Plot Mars's position around the Sun with his sines, interpolating between entries as Indian astronomers did, and measure how far off the spacecraft would aim.

Gold: his sines minus true sines, magnified 1,000 times. Dots: the 24 table entries themselves.

Idea 4 · Golapada 5 and 37

Moonlight is sunlight; eclipses are shadows

He wrote that half of the Earth, the planets and the stars is dark with their own shadow and the half turned to the Sun is lit (Golapada 5), and that the Moon hides the Sun and the great shadow of the Earth hides the Moon (Golapada 37), not a demon swallowing them. A century later Brahmagupta attacked the verse and defended the demon Rahu (Brahmasphutasiddhanta XI.9). Aryabhata was right.

Spaceflight test. Lunar landers plan around sunlight: a south-pole landing needs the Sun up and the lighting repeats every synodic month. Predict the next full moons from his counts (Moon 57,753,336 and Sun 4,320,000 trips round the sky per yuga, both starting together at the Kali Yuga) and compare with the modern mean lunar theory.

Quiz: five questions

Answer each one by using the lab above, then check. Good for a class: pairs work through them, the teacher reveals the answers.

  1. Aryabhata found π with four steps of arithmetic. What are the steps, and what value do they give?

    Use the lab: Read Idea 2 · Ganitapada 10, “Π from four steps of arithmetic”.

    Show answer

    Add 4 to 100, multiply by 8, add 62,000: (100 + 4) × 8 + 62,000 = 62,832. That is the circumference of a circle 20,000 across, so π ≈ 62,832 ÷ 20,000 = 3.1416. He called it approximate; it is off by only 7 parts in 10 million.

  2. Using Aryabhata's rotation period, which launch site gives an eastbound rocket the most free speed, and which gives the least?

    Use the lab: In Idea 1, click each launch-site button (Sriharikota, Kourou, Boca Chica, Kennedy, Baikonur) and read the free speed.

    Show answer

    Most: Kourou at 5.24° N, 0.463 km/s. Least: Baikonur at 45.96° N, 0.323 km/s. Sriharikota gives 0.452 km/s. Sites nearer the equator move faster as the Earth turns.

  3. Aryabhata's sine table was good enough for the naked eye. Is it good enough to aim a spacecraft at Mars?

    Use the lab: In Idea 3 · Dasagitika 12, read the Test / Result table and the verdict under it.

    Show answer

    No. His worst error is about 1.7 arc-minutes, close to the sharpest a human eye can see. But aiming at Mars with it would miss by up to 112,005 km, about 16.5 Mars diameters.

  4. Aryabhata said eclipses are shadows, not a demon. Why can the Moon be fully hidden in a lunar eclipse?

    Use the lab: Read the similar-triangles result at the end of Idea 4 · Golapada 5 and 37.

    Show answer

    The Earth's shadow is about 1,381,349 km long, 3.6 times the Moon's distance. Where the Moon crosses it, the shadow is still about 9,196 km wide, wider than the Moon (3,475 km). So the Moon can sit fully inside it, and total lunar eclipses happen.

  5. HistoryFrom his yuga counts, how long did Aryabhata say the Earth takes to turn once against the stars, and how close is that to the modern value?

    Use the lab: In Idea 1, read the table One turn against the stars.

    Show answer

    Aryabhata: 86,164.101 s (23 h 56 m 4.10 s). Modern atomic-clock value: 86,164.091 s (23 h 56 m 4.09 s). The difference is only 0.011 s, 1 part in about 8 million. It comes from 1,582,237,500 Earth turns in a yuga of 4,320,000 years.
