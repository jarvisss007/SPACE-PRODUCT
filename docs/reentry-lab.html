Re-entry Lab

Coming home is the most violent part of a space flight. A capsule from the Moon hits the top of the air at 11 km/s, and the air has to take away almost all of that speed in a few minutes. Come in too shallow and the capsule skips back out to space. Come in too steep and the braking crushes the crew and the heat burns through the shield. Pick a capsule and try to thread the gap.

How far to trust it

The capsules

Mass and diameter set how much air a capsule needs to slow down (its “ballistic coefficient”). Lift lets it steer: by rolling, the crew points the lift up, down or sideways, which is how Apollo, Soyuz and Orion keep the g-load low and land near the ships. Numbers marked “assumed” are typical values for that kind of capsule, not published figures.

What the model leaves out

The flight is computed in one plane over a round Earth that does not spin, through the US Standard Atmosphere 1976. The drag coefficient and lift are held constant at their hypersonic values, so the last few kilometres before the parachutes are only rough. Heating is the Sutton–Graves estimate at the stagnation point from the air flowing straight onto the shield. It leaves out the glowing gas’s own radiation, which matters above about 10 km/s, so the fastest entries are hotter than shown. The autopilot is a simple rule, not Apollo’s actual guidance.
