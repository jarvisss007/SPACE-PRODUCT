A what-if, computed

Starship Through Time

Put a modern Starship in the hands of India's classical astronomers, at their own observatory towns, in their own century, and fly it to Mars on their numbers: their planet clock picks the launch date, their trigonometry aims it, their units keep time. Everything about the rocket is modern; everything about the navigation is theirs. The outcome below is calculated, not imagined. The story around it is labelled as a story.

In simple words

What if India’s ancient astronomers had a modern Starship rocket? This page flies it to Mars using only their own numbers to pick the launch day and aim the ship, and works out how close they would really get.

Try this first: Click one of the four era cards, starting with Aryabhata (499 CE), and watch the flight. Then press Navigator sights Mars mid-cruise to let the crew look at Mars halfway and fix their aim.

  • Aryabhata’s numbers picked a launch day less than a week away from the best day a modern model finds, more than 1,500 years ago.
  • Even so, every ancient ship arrives millions of kilometres from Mars, because Mars really moves on a slightly squashed, oval path, not a perfect circle.
  • A navigator who spots Mars halfway there can fire the engines to correct the aim, at the cost of extra fuel.

Why the clocks miss: they track the average motion of the planets very well (Mars's year to a few hours in 687 days), but Mars's real orbit is noticeably oval, so its true position swings up to about 10° either side of the average. The siddhantas did have correction rules for this (the manda correction); this page flies on the mean motions alone, the part of their work that is a pure count of revolutions. The aiming error from Aryabhata's sine table (up to 112,000 km at Mars) is small beside it.

What would have happened: the story

Quiz: five questions

Answer each one by using the lab above, then check. Good for a class: pairs work through them, the teacher reveals the answers.

  1. Flying only on Aryabhata's planet clock, how far from Mars does his 499 CE Starship arrive?

    Use the lab: Click the 499 CE Aryabhata card and read Distance off at arrival and the line under it.

    Show answer

    About 12.1 million km off, roughly 1,789 times the width of Mars. His clock puts Mars about 3.05° from where it really is.

  2. Which of the four eras would come closest to Mars, and which clock does it use?

    Use the lab: Click each era card (499, 550, 628, 640 CE), or read the table All four eras side by side.

    Show answer

    Nalanda, 640 CE, using Brahmagupta's clock (the newest of the day): Mars error 2.36°, a miss of 9.4 M km, needing a 0.84 km/s fix. Aryabhata's 499 CE mission misses most, by 12.1 M km.

  3. How can Aryabhata's ship still reach Mars, and what does the fix cost?

    Use the lab: Pick the 499 CE Aryabhata card and click Navigator sights Mars mid-cruise. Read the line under Distance off at arrival.

    Show answer

    Halfway out a navigator sights Mars about 3.0° from where the Aryabhatiya said and burns 1.08 km/s to correct. That costs about 96 t more propellant for a ship carrying 100 t to Mars: one more tanker flight before departure.

  4. The old texts track Mars's average motion very well. So why do the clocks still miss Mars by millions of kilometres?

    Use the lab: Read the paragraph starting Why the clocks miss under the four-era table.

    Show answer

    Mars's real orbit is noticeably oval, so its true position swings up to about 10° either side of the average. This page flies on the mean motions alone. The siddhantas did have a correction for this (the manda correction), but it is not used here.

  5. HistoryWhich launch date did the Aryabhatiya pick for the Mars trip, and how far is it from the modern model's best date?

    Use the lab: Click the 499 CE Aryabhata card and read Departure picked by the Aryabhatiya and Modern model's best date.

    Show answer

    The Aryabhatiya picks 24 Jan 500 CE; the modern model picks 18 Jan 500 CE. That is only 6.6 days apart, more than 1,500 years ago, from a pure count of planet revolutions.

The “modern” comparison is the same circular, coplanar mean-motion model used there (J2000 elements), not a full ephemeris, so the misses measure the ancient counts against modern counts. Errors are computed frame-free, as Mars at arrival relative to Earth at departure, so the different zero points of the Indian and modern zodiacs cancel. Time units: 1 ghatika = 24 min, 1 vinadi = 24 s, 1 prana ≈ 4 s; a Nalanda water-clock sinking at the equinox is about 45 min (Yijing). Dates before 1582 are in the Julian calendar.
