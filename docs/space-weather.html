Space Weather Now

The Sun is never quiet. It flings out X-ray flashes that reach Earth in eight minutes, a steady wind of charged particles, and now and then a billion-tonne cloud of magnetised gas. When that cloud hits Earth it can light up auroras, knock out radio and GPS, and strain power grids. This page shows what the Sun is doing right now, from the same spacecraft forecasters use.

The solar wind

The Sun blows a wind of charged particles past Earth all the time. Spacecraft parked 1.5 million km sunward, at the L1 balance point, taste it first. The faster it blows, the less warning we get. When its magnetic field points south (Bz below zero) it links with Earth’s and pours energy in. That is when storms grow.

Geomagnetic storms: the last week and the next three days

Kp measures how hard Earth’s magnetic field is being shaken, from 0 (calm) to 9 (extreme), every three hours. Solid bars are measured; faded bars are NOAA’s forecast. Kp 5 and above is a storm (G1 to G5). In the G5 storm of May 2024, the strongest in two decades, aurora was seen from northern India.

India’s eye on the Sun: Aditya-L1

Aditya-L1, launched on 2 September 2023, reached its orbit around the L1 balance point on 6 January 2024. It circles that empty point once every 177.86 days, about 1.5 million km from Earth, with an unbroken view of the Sun. Two of its seven instruments, ASPEX and PAPA, sample the solar wind itself, the same wind charted above, before it reaches Earth. VELC and SUIT photograph the Sun’s outer atmosphere, where these storm clouds are born. The live readings on this page come from NOAA’s spacecraft at L1. Aditya-L1’s data is released through ISRO’s own archive, not as a live feed. See its orbit in the Lagrange Point Lab.

What this page leaves out

All the readings are NOAA’s and NASA’s, shown as they arrive. This page does not forecast anything itself; the Kp forecast is NOAA’s. The storm launcher is the drag-based model of Vršnak and others (2013), with typical drag values of 0.1 and 0.2 × 10⁻⁷ per km. It is aimed straight at Earth and starts at 20 solar radii, where coronagraphs first measure a cloud’s speed. Real clouds expand, twist, hit slower clouds and arrive off-centre, so forecasts made this way are typically off by about half a day. The warning time at L1 assumes the cloud keeps its arrival speed over the last 1.5 million km.

Quiz: five questions

Answer each one by using the lab above, then check. Good for a class: pairs work through them, the teacher reveals the answers.

  1. How many times brighter in X-rays is an X-class flare than a C-class flare?

    Use the lab: Read the note under the X-ray flashes, last 24 hours chart.

    Show answer

    100 times. Each step up the scale (A, B, C, M, X) is ten times brighter, and X is two steps above C: 10 × 10 = 100.

  2. For the fastest storm cloud ever measured, how much does a light cloud differ from a big heavy cloud?

    Use the lab: In Launch a solar storm, drag Solar wind it ploughs through to 400 km/s. Click Fastest measured with Big heavy cloud selected and read Reaches Earth in and Arrives at. Then click Light cloud.

    Show answer

    Heavy cloud: about 19.3 h, arriving at about 1,352 km/s. Light cloud: about 28.2 h, arriving at about 818 km/s. The wind drags harder on a light cloud, so it slows down more.

  3. How do you work out the warning time that the spacecraft at L1 gives us? Use the May 2024 storm as an example.

    Use the lab: Read the text in The solar wind section, then find the May 2024 row in the How far to trust it table.

    Show answer

    Warning time = 1,500,000 km ÷ wind speed. In May 2024 the wind blew at 750–950 km/s, giving only about 26–33 minutes of warning. Faster wind means less warning.

  4. Why are storms more likely when the Bz tile shows a number below zero?

    Use the lab: Read the Magnetic field Bz tile and the text in The solar wind section.

    Show answer

    When Bz is below zero, the wind's magnetic field points south. It links with Earth's field and pours energy in: the page says the door is open. When it points north, the door is shut.

  5. HistoryIn 1859 the Carrington storm cloud reached Earth in 17.6 hours. According to the model, how fast would a cloud need to be launched to do that in normal solar wind, and what does that tell us?

    Use the lab: Find the Carrington 1859 row in the How far to trust it table. Compare it with the Fastest measured button value.

    Show answer

    About 3,580 km/s, faster than any cloud ever measured (3,200 km/s). So the path was probably cleared by an earlier cloud that went first.
