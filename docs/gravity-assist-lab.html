Gravity Assist Lab

Flying from Earth to Neptune on the cheapest path, with no help on the way, takes 30.6 years. Voyager 2 got there in twelve by flying close past Jupiter, Saturn and Uranus, and stealing a little of each planet’s speed around the Sun. Here those flybys are rebuilt from JPL’s planet positions and nothing but the dates the spacecraft arrived. If the physics is right, it should tell us how close Voyager had to pass each planet. It does.

Speed around the Sun

Coasting outward, a spacecraft slows down, just as a ball thrown upward does. Each flyby is a jump. When the line crosses the dashed curve, the spacecraft is going fast enough to leave the Sun for ever.

The test: how close did they have to pass?

For each flyby, the model works out the spacecraft’s path in from the previous planet and out to the next one, using only the arrival dates. The bend between the two paths has to come from the planet’s gravity, and a sharper bend needs a closer pass. The model never sees the real closest-approach distances; it predicts them.

Distances are from the planet’s centre. “Mismatch” is the engine push the model would need to stitch the two paths together. The real flybys needed almost none, so a small mismatch means the model is close to reality. Voyager 1’s Saturn flyby bent it up out of the planets’ plane towards Titan, so the model cannot follow it there.

Try one flyby

In the planet’s own frame, a flyby only bends the path; the spacecraft leaves exactly as fast as it arrived. But the planet is moving around the Sun, and seen from the Sun that bend turns into a change of speed. Passing behind the planet adds speed; passing in front takes it away.

When is the next Grand Tour?

In 1964 Gary Flandro at JPL noticed that Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune would line up in the late 1970s so one spacecraft could visit all four, and that this happens only about once every 175 years. We checked him. For every year from 1950 to 2250, the lab tried thousands of launch dates and flight times. Each tour had to launch with no more push than Voyager had, reach Neptune within 16 years, and get every bend from gravity alone. The lines show the extra engine push the best tour found that year would still need; shaded bands mark years where a tour works on gravity alone. Tap a year to see its best tour.

What the model leaves out

These are patched conics: the Sun alone steers the spacecraft between planets, and each planet acts only at the moment of the flyby. The paths between planets are solved exactly (Lambert’s problem) for the real dates, but the small course corrections the real spacecraft made along the way are not included. Planet positions come from JPL’s approximate elements, which are good to a few hundredths of a degree. That is close enough to land the predicted distances within 5% of the real ones. The search for future tours uses a grid of launch dates and flight times, then refines the best few, so a better tour may exist in a year it marks as poor.

Quiz: five questions

Answer each one by using the lab above, then check. Good for a class: pairs work through them, the teacher reveals the answers.

  1. How much did Voyager 2's speed around the Sun change at Jupiter?

    Use the lab: Voyager 2 is picked at the start. Read Jupiter flyby: speed around the Sun under the speed chart.

    Show answer

    From 9.6 to 19.7 km/s, a gain of about 10 km/s with no fuel burned. The chart labels it JUPITER +10.0.

  2. In "Try one flyby", what changes when you pass in front of Jupiter instead of behind it?

    Use the lab: Keep Jupiter, 7.9 km/s and 10.10 radii. Read the verdict. Then drag Which side to In front of the planet.

    Show answer

    Behind the planet: speed around the Sun goes 11.4 to 20.5 km/s, a gain of 9.1 km/s. In front: 11.4 to 8.0 km/s, a loss of 3.4 km/s. The path is bent by 95.1° both times; only the side changes.

  3. Which mission got the biggest boost from Jupiter: Voyager 1, Voyager 2 or New Horizons?

    Use the lab: Click each mission button in turn and read Jupiter flyby: speed around the Sun.

    Show answer

    Voyager 1: 12.7 to 23.6 km/s (about +10.8). Voyager 2: 9.6 to 19.7 (+10.0). New Horizons: 19.1 to 22.1 (only +3.0). The test table shows New Horizons passed about 2.3 million km from Jupiter, far out, so its path bent much less.

  4. Why is the closest-approach table called a "test" of the physics?

    Use the lab: Read The test: how close did they have to pass? and compare the Real closest approach and Model needs columns.

    Show answer

    The model is given only the arrival dates, never the real distances, and it predicts them. For Voyager 2 at Saturn it predicts 159,900 km against the real 161,000 km, only 0.7% off. Predicting a number it never saw shows the gravity model is right.

  5. HistoryWho spotted the rare line-up that made the Grand Tour possible, and when is the next chance?

    Use the lab: Read When is the next Grand Tour? and the box under the chart (it opens on 1977). Then click the chart near 2155.

    Show answer

    Gary Flandro at JPL noticed it in 1964; it happens only about once every 175 years. For 1977 the box says a Grand Tour works. The shaded bands show tours on gravity alone only in the late 1970s and early 1980s, and then around 2155, when the box says it works only by threading Saturn, passing inside its rings.
