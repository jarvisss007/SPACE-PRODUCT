In simple words

Every planet ever confirmed around another star, on one chart. Click a dot to meet that world, watch it pass in front of its star, and see how long a trip there would take.

Try this first: Type TRAPPIST-1 in the search box at the top and click one of its planets. Then scroll to Watch it transit to see it cross in front of its star.

  • Telescopes find most of these planets by spotting the tiny dip in starlight when a planet passes in front of its star.
  • A small planet around a tiny star blocks much more light than Earth blocks of the Sun.
  • A planet that is Earth-sized and gets Earth-like sunlight is only a first guess. It does not mean the planet has life, or even water.

Watch it transit

Rocky size, Earth-like starlight

A simple filter: planets between 0.5 and 1.6 Earth radii receiving between a quarter and one and a half times the starlight Earth gets. This is a first sort, not a finding of life or even of water: a planet here could be airless, scorched by flares, or locked with one face to its star.

Quiz: five questions

Answer each one by using the lab above, then check. Good for a class: pairs work through them, the teacher reveals the answers.

  1. The page opens on Proxima Cen b. How far away is it, and how was it found?

    Use the lab: Read the planet panel on the right (the big name shows Proxima Cen b). Look at Distance and Found by.

    Show answer

    About 4.2 light-years. It was found by Radial Velocity in 2016, so the note warns that its radius is an estimate from its mass.

  2. Kepler-22 b gets almost the same starlight as Earth. Why is it not in the "Rocky size, Earth-like starlight" table?

    Use the lab: Type Kepler-22 in the search box and click Kepler-22 b. Read Radius and Starlight received. Then read the rule above the table.

    Show answer

    Its starlight is about 1.01 × Earth, which fits. But its radius is about 2.10 × Earth. The table only takes planets between 0.5 and 1.6 Earth radii, so it is too big to count as rocky size.

  3. Compare the trip to Proxima Cen b with the trip to TRAPPIST-1 e on a 1 g ship.

    Use the lab: Read 1 g ship, crew time for Proxima Cen b. Then search TRAPPIST-1, click TRAPPIST-1 e and read the same row and its Distance.

    Show answer

    Proxima Cen b: about 3.5 years for the crew (4.2 light-years). TRAPPIST-1 e: about 7.3 years (about 40.5 light-years). The distance is nearly 10 times bigger, but the crew time only doubles, because the ship keeps speeding up for half the trip, and relativity makes the crew age far less than people at home.

  4. TRAPPIST-1 e is smaller than Earth. Why does it dim its star far more than Earth dims the Sun?

    Use the lab: Select TRAPPIST-1 e and read the note under Watch it transit. Compare its dip with the sentence about Earth crossing the Sun.

    Show answer

    It blocks about 0.50% of the light. Earth blocks the Sun by only 84 ppm (0.0084%). The dip is (planet radius ÷ star radius)², and TRAPPIST-1 is a tiny star, only about 0.12 solar radii. So a small planet covers a big part of it.

  5. HistoryLook up 51 Peg b. How and when was it found, and how does the discoveries chart show that history?

    Use the lab: Search 51 Peg and click 51 Peg b. Read Found by. Then look at the Discoveries by year chart and its label.

    Show answer

    51 Peg b was found by Radial Velocity in 1995. The discoveries chart starts in 1992 with only a few planets a year. Its tallest bar is labelled 2016 (Kepler): the peak year, credited to the Kepler telescope.
