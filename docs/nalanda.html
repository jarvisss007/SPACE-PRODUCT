Nalanda Mahavihara

नालन्दा महाविहार

For seven centuries Nalanda was the largest centre of learning in Asia: a walled monastery of brick halls and temples where monks studied logic, grammar, medicine, philosophy and the arts, and where pilgrims walked from China to study. Two of them, Xuanzang (there c. 637–642) and Yijing (c. 675–685), wrote down what they saw. Their accounts are the backbone of this page.

Much of what is repeated about Nalanda today goes beyond those accounts. Every claim below carries a tag: Evidence for what a primary source or a surviving object shows, Pilgrim's figure for numbers given by an eyewitness that historians question, Legend for stories with no early source.

The water clock that ran the day

A thin copper bowl with a tiny hole floats in a larger vessel; water seeps in until it sinks, and each sinking is struck on a drum.Yijing, A Record of the Buddhist Religion, ch. XXX (Takakusu 1896, pp. 144–146), paraphrased. The bowl held two sheng, “about two pints”, and the hole was sized “according to the time of the year”.

Yijing describes the code exactly: one, two and three strokes for the first three sinkings; at the fourth, four strokes, two blasts of a conch and one more stroke, marking the first hour. Four more sinkings and the drum gives four strokes, a conch and two strokes: noon. Monks did not eat after noon. Four hours by day and four by night make eight, 32 sinkings in all. Boys tended the clocks, which were gifts from kings. Xuanzang independently says Indians divide day and night into eight parts.

Rebuilt here: the clock runs on Nalanda's real day length for the date you choose, and the calculator works out how big the pin-hole must be. The hole size is our physics, not Yijing's: water entering under a few centimetres of head, a standard flow law.

What the sources say, and what they don't

Books that survived

Nalanda's scribes signed their work. A handful of palm-leaf manuscripts carry colophons naming Nalanda and the Pala king's regnal year, and several lost works by Nalanda scholars resurfaced in the 19th and 20th centuries in Tibet and in a Jain library in Rajasthan.

The school

Yijing names the five sciences (vidya): grammar, arts and crafts, medicine, logic and the inner science. Hui Li adds that the monks also studied the Vedas, the Atharvaveda and Samkhya alongside all eighteen Buddhist schools. Astronomy is not on any pilgrim's list. Before Nalanda came years of grammar; Yijing gives the ages.

Age 6Siddhirastu primer, the alphabet and its 10,000 combinations, 6 months
Age 8Panini's sutras, 1,000 verses, learned in 8 months
Age 10Verb roots and the three supplements, about 3 years
Age 15The Kasika commentary, 5 years
ThenBhartrhari, logic and Abhidharma, then 2–3 years at Nalanda or Valabhi

Medicine, per Yijing: a physician read the patient's voice and face and treated by eight branches (wounds, the head, the body, possession, poisons, children, long life, vigour), summarised into one handbook used across India. His first rule was fasting: up to a week in Central India.

An idea to try: Dignaga's wheel of reasons

Nalanda's logicians asked when a reason actually proves a claim. Dignaga's answer was a 3 × 3 grid. Take “the hill has fire, because it has smoke”. Look at cases that are like the claim (places with fire) and cases that are not (places without fire), and ask whether the reason, smoke, is found in all, some or none of each. Only two of the nine boxes give a valid reason. Dharmakirti, who studied this tradition, went on to argue that scripture may not override what perception and reasoning can settle. Click a box.
