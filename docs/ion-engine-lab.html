NASA’s Dawn · xenon · the gentlest engine that goes the furthest

Ion Engine Lab

A chemical rocket burns fuel and throws the hot gas out at about 3 km/s. An ion engine uses solar electricity to fling single xenon atoms out at over 30 km/s, ten times faster, so each kilogram of propellant gives ten times the push. The catch is that it throws very little at a time. Dawn’s engine pushed with 90 millinewtons, about the weight of a sheet of paper resting on your hand. Yet over eleven years it gave Dawn more change in speed than any spacecraft before it, enough to orbit two different worlds, Vesta and then Ceres.

Spiral out of Earth orbit

A chemical rocket leaves Earth orbit in one short, hard burn at the lowest point. An ion engine cannot do that: it pushes for months, so the spacecraft spirals slowly outwards. Pick the engine’s push and watch. The surprise is how much more speed change the slow spiral needs.

What the model leaves out

The rocket equation here assumes all the propellant goes through one engine at one efficiency. Dawn also carried hydrazine for steering, and its solar power, and with it the ion engine’s push, fell as it moved away from the Sun. The spiral starts from a circular 400 km orbit, pushes always along the direction of motion, and ignores Earth’s shadow, the Moon and air drag. The chemical comparison uses a typical specific impulse of 320 s.

Quiz: five questions

Answer each one by using the lab above, then check. Good for a class: pairs work through them, the teacher reveals the answers.

  1. How fast does Dawn's ion engine throw out its xenon?

    Use the lab: Read the first row of the Dawn, re-derived card, Exhaust speed (3,100 s × g).

    Show answer

    30.4 km/s. That is about ten times faster than the hot gas from a chemical rocket (about 3 km/s).

  2. For a change in speed of 5 km/s, how much propellant does each engine need?

    Use the lab: Drag the Change in speed wanted slider to 5.00 km/s. Read the ION and CHEMICAL labels on the chart.

    Show answer

    Ion: about 134 kg. Chemical: about 2,929 kg, for the same 747 kg spacecraft. The chemical engine needs about 22 times more.

  3. How long does a spiral out of Earth orbit take, and what changes if the push is gentler?

    Use the lab: In Spiral out of Earth orbit, keep the push at 1.0 mm/s² and press Fly the spiral. Then drag the push slider fully left (100 µm/s²) and press Fly the spiral again.

    Show answer

    At 1.0 mm/s²: about 81 days, 345 laps and 7.03 km/s of speed change. At 100 µm/s²: about 2.3 years, 3,452 laps and 7.31 km/s. One quick chemical burn would need only 3.18 km/s.

  4. Why could Dawn stop and orbit two worlds when a chemical spacecraft could only fly past?

    Use the lab: Read the verdict box in the Dawn, re-derived card.

    Show answer

    A chemical spacecraft as heavy as Dawn would need about 28,300 kg of propellant for the same 11.49 km/s. That is 67 times the 425 kg of xenon Dawn carried. Each kilogram of xenon gives about ten times the push, so Dawn had enough to stop at Vesta and then Ceres.

  5. HistoryWho worked out the limit for a slow spiral escape, and when? What does the limit say?

    Use the lab: Read the verdict under the spiral and the third row of the Checks table.

    Show answer

    T. N. Edelbaum, in 1961. The gentler the push, the closer the speed change gets to the whole starting orbital speed. From a 400 km orbit that is 7.67 km/s; the lab gets 7.03 km/s at 1 mm/s².
