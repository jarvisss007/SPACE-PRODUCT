Tonight's Sky

Pick your place, and this page works out what you can see tonight with your own eyes: which planets are up and where to look, how full the Moon is, and when the space station crosses your sky. Everything is calculated here in your browser from planetary orbits and public satellite data.

Quiz: five questions

  1. How close is this page's Full Moon time to the published time for November 2026?

    Published: 24 November 2026, 14:53 UTC. The page is off by about 30 minutes, good enough for finding things by eye.

  2. On the night of 24 November 2026 in Delhi, how full is the Moon and when does it rise?

    Use the lab: Set Your place to Delhi and Night of to 24 November 2026. Read The Moon card and Sunset in The night.

    Show answer

    Full Moon, 100% lit. It rises around 4:55 pm, close to sunset (about 5:24 pm). A bright Moon washes out faint stars that night.

  3. On 21 December 2026, whose night is longer: Srinagar's or Thiruvananthapuram's?

    Use the lab: Set Night of to 21 December 2026. Pick Srinagar, then Thiruvananthapuram, and compare Sunset and Sunrise in The night.

    Show answer

    Srinagar: sunset about 5:25 pm, sunrise about 7:32 am, about 14 hours. Thiruvananthapuram: about 6:09 pm to 6:31 am, about 12.4 hours. In December, places further north have longer nights.

  4. On 21 June 2027, the page says London is never fully dark. Why? Compare with Delhi.

    Use the lab: Set Night of to 21 June 2027. Pick London, then Delhi, and read Fully dark in The night.

    Show answer

    London shows never fully dark. Delhi is fully dark from about 8:56 pm. London is far north (51.5°N), so in midsummer the Sun never sinks far enough below the horizon. Twilight lasts all night.

  5. HistoryThis page uses work done by earlier astronomers. Which star catalogue gives the bright stars' positions, and whose formulas give the planets' brightness?

    Use the lab: Read the footer at the bottom of the page.

    Show answer

    Star positions come from the Hipparcos catalogue. Planet brightness uses the formulas of Mallama and Hilton (2018). Planet orbits come from JPL's approximate elements (Standish and Williams).

Planets from JPL’s approximate orbital elements (Standish and Williams, Table 2a). The Moon from the low-precision series in the Astronomical Almanac. Planet brightness from the standard formulas of Mallama and Hilton (2018), simplified. Bright stars’ J2000 positions from the Hipparcos catalogue. Satellites from CelesTrak’s public orbit data, propagated with satellite.js 5.0.0 (MIT); the snapshot date is shown on the Satellite Watch. Expect positions good to about a degree, which is plenty for finding things by eye.
