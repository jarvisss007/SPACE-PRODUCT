Earth from Above

A million and a half kilometres away, near the balance point between Earth and the Sun, a NASA camera called EPIC photographs the whole sunlit face of our planet about a dozen times a day. These are those photographs, loaded straight from NASA as you open the page. Play the day and watch Earth turn. The first photograph of the whole round Earth, the “Blue Marble”, was a single snapshot taken by the Apollo 17 crew on 7 December 1972; DSCOVR, launched in February 2015, now takes one every couple of hours.

Where the camera is

Seen from above Earth’s north pole, not to scale for Earth and Moon sizes. DSCOVR sits almost on the line from Earth to the Sun, which is why every photograph shows a fully lit Earth.

The whole Earth, stitched

One camera never sees all of Earth at once. So here every photograph from one day is laid back onto the globe where the camera saw it, and each spot is taken from the picture that looked at it most squarely. Turn it to see the side facing away from you in the photos above.

Zoom in past 3× and the picture sharpens. That detail comes from a second NASA source: the VIIRS camera on the NOAA-20 weather satellite, which flies only 824 km up and passes over every place around 1:30 in the afternoon, local time. Its clouds are from a different hour than EPIC’s, so they may not match. Thin black wedges near the equator are gaps between its passes.

Why some parts look odd. The photographs are about two hours apart, so clouds can jump a little where two pictures meet. The dark cap is polar night: in November the Sun does not rise near the North Pole, and EPIC, which always looks from the Sun’s side, cannot see it. In June the dark cap moves to the south.

Then and now

From a grainy rocket snapshot to a camera that photographs the whole planet every day. Tap a year to open it; green dots mark India’s part.

  1. 1946The first photo from space

    On 24 October 1946 a film camera rode a captured German V-2 rocket up from New Mexico to about 105 km. The film survived the crash in a steel box, and showed Earth’s curve in black and white.

  2. 1959Explorer 6: the first picture from orbit

    On 14 August 1959 the US satellite Explorer 6 sent back a fuzzy image of sunlit clouds over the Pacific. It was crude, but it was the first view of Earth from a satellite going round it.

  3. 1968Earthrise

    On 24 December 1968 the Apollo 8 astronauts, circling the Moon, saw Earth rise over its grey horizon. Bill Anders’s photo made many people realise how small and alone our planet is.

  4. 1972The Blue Marble

    On 7 December 1972 the Apollo 17 crew photographed the whole sunlit Earth from about 29,000 km away. For decades it was the picture of our planet; the camera on this page now takes one like it every day.

  5. 1972Landsat starts mapping the land

    Landsat 1, launched on 23 July 1972, was the first satellite built to photograph land for farmers, foresters and map-makers. Landsat satellites are still at work today, giving the longest unbroken record of Earth’s surface from space.

  6. 1979Bhaskara-IIndia

    India’s first Earth-observation satellite went up on 7 June 1979 on a Soviet rocket. Its TV cameras tested how India could watch its own land, water and forests from space.

  7. 1988IRS-1AIndia

    Launched on 17 March 1988, IRS-1A was the first of India’s Indian Remote Sensing satellites. It began the work ISRO still does: mapping crops, droughts, floods and cities.

  8. 1990The Pale Blue Dot

    On 14 February 1990 Voyager 1, about 6 billion km away, turned round and photographed Earth. Our whole world showed up as a speck smaller than one pixel.

  9. 2005Cartosat-1India

    Launched by PSLV on 5 May 2005, Cartosat-1 took pairs of pictures from two angles at once, so maps could be made in 3D. Later Cartosats can see objects less than a metre across.

  10. 2015DSCOVR and EPIC: this page’s camera

    DSCOVR launched in February 2015 to a spot 1.5 million km away where it always sees the sunlit side, and EPIC began sending daily full-Earth photos that year. It went quiet for about eight months in 2019–20 while engineers fixed a fault, but it is still working: when this was written in November 2026, its newest picture was from 16 November 2026.

  11. 2025–26NISAR: radar that sees through cloudsIndia

    NASA and ISRO’s NISAR, launched on India’s GSLV on 30 July 2025, uses radar to spot the ground rising or sinking by about a centimetre, through clouds and in the dark. It began its science work in late 2025, its first data opened to everyone in July 2026, and it marked one year in orbit in August 2026.

For experts: Checks

How to read the pictures

These are natural-colour images: close to what your eye would see. The point at the very centre of each picture is the spot on Earth directly below the camera. Because the camera stays nearly fixed while Earth spins, each new picture is centred about 15° further west for every hour that has passed. Pictures appear on NASA’s site a day or two after they are taken, after processing. If they do not load, NASA’s server may be busy or you may be offline; the original archive is at epic.gsfc.nasa.gov.

Quiz: five questions

Answer each one by using the lab above, then check. Good for a class: pairs work through them, the teacher reveals the answers.

  1. About how far from Earth is the EPIC camera?

    Use the lab: Read the first row, Camera's distance from Earth, in the Checks table.

    Show answer

    About 1.5 million km, near the L1 balance point between Earth and the Sun. DSCOVR loops around L1 rather than sitting exactly on it.

  2. How far does the centre of the picture move each hour, and in which direction? If one picture is centred at 80°E, where is a picture taken 2 hours later centred?

    Use the lab: Press Spin the day and watch Directly under the camera change. Read the How fast the view turns row in the Checks table and the text in How to read the pictures.

    Show answer

    About 15° west every hour, because Earth turns once in 24 hours (360° ÷ 24 = 15°). Two hours later the centre is about 80° − 30° = 50°E.

  3. How long does light take to travel from Earth to the camera?

    Use the lab: Read Camera's distance from Earth and Light from Earth to camera in the details box. Check the rule with the numbers.

    Show answer

    About 5 seconds. Time = distance ÷ speed of light. For 1,500,000 km: 1,500,000 ÷ 299,792 km/s ≈ 5.0 s.

  4. Why does every EPIC photograph show a fully lit Earth, never a half-lit one?

    Use the lab: Read Angle between camera and Sun, seen from Earth in the details box and look at the Where the camera is map.

    Show answer

    DSCOVR sits almost on the line from Earth to the Sun, so the angle is small. The camera looks at Earth from nearly the Sun's direction, so it sees the whole sunlit face.

  5. HistoryThe first photograph of the whole round Earth was taken in 1972. Who took it, and how is today’s picture different?

    Use the lab: Read the introduction at the top of the page, then press Spin the day.

    Show answer

    The Apollo 17 crew took the “Blue Marble” on 7 December 1972, on their way to the Moon: a single snapshot. Since 2015 the EPIC camera on DSCOVR has photographed the whole sunlit Earth about a dozen times every day, so you can watch a full day turn.
