Asteroid Watch

Every week small asteroids slip past Earth, some closer than the Moon and a few closer than our weather satellites. Most are found only days before, or after. Every few days somewhere, one of them hits the air and explodes as a fireball. This page shows the real list from NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory: who passed, who is coming, where the sky lit up, and which known asteroids still have any chance at all of hitting us.

In simple words

Every week small asteroids fly past Earth, some closer than the Moon. This page shows NASA's real list, updated every day.

Try this first: Tap any circle on the first chart to see that asteroid's size, speed and how close it came. Then try Chelyabinsk 2013.

  • Most asteroids that pass close are small, from a car to a building, and many are found only days before or after.
  • Small ones burn up in the air as bright fireballs. That happens somewhere on Earth every few weeks.
  • No known large asteroid is going to hit Earth in the next 100 years. Apophis passes very close in 2029, safely.

Fireballs

US government sensors see the flash when a rock a metre or more across explodes in the air. Each dot is one, sized by its energy. Most fall over the ocean, and nobody notices.

Where the sky lit up

The risk list

NASA’s Sentry system follows every known asteroid that cannot yet be ruled out as a future impactor. Most have tiny odds and drop off the list as their orbits are measured better. The Palermo scale compares each risk with the everyday background chance of an impact: below −2 means no likely consequences; −2 to 0 merits careful watching.

Apophis, 13 April 2029

In 2004 the 340–370 m asteroid Apophis briefly had a 2.7% chance of hitting Earth in 2029, the highest Torino rating ever given. Better orbits ruled the impact out. But it will still pass closer than the satellites that bring you TV and weather pictures, bright enough to see with the naked eye. NASA’s OSIRIS-APEX and the European–Japanese Ramses are planned to visit it.

    Widths are estimated from each asteroid’s brightness (its absolute magnitude H) with the standard formula D = 1329 km ÷ √p × 10−H/5. The reflectivity p is unknown for most, so the page shows the range for p from 0.25 (shiny) to 0.05 (dark), with 0.14 in the middle. Real widths can fall outside it. Impact energy is the energy of motion only: it does not model how deep a rock gets before it breaks up, the crater or the blast. Close-approach distances are from Earth’s centre. The data is a daily snapshot: newly found asteroids appear only at the next refresh.

    Quiz: five questions

    Answer each one by using the lab above, then check. Good for a class: pairs work through them, the teacher reveals the answers.

    1. How close will Apophis come on 13 April 2029, and is that closer than the satellites that carry TV and weather pictures?

      Use the lab: Read the cards under Apophis, 13 April 2029 and look at the picture beside them.

      Show answer

      About 31,640 km above the surface, from JPL’s distance of 38,011 km from Earth’s centre. Geostationary satellites orbit at 35,793 km, so Apophis passes inside their ring.

    2. Using the energy bench, how much energy did the Chelyabinsk rock carry, and why does a rock twice as wide carry so much more?

      Use the lab: Click Chelyabinsk 2013 under What if it hit?, read Energy, then double the width.

      Show answer

      About 400 kilotons of TNT, matching the published 400–500 kt. Twice the width means 8 times the volume and mass, so 8 times the energy at the same speed.

    3. Why does the chart of passes look crowded in the past month and nearly empty in the months ahead?

      Use the lab: Look at the Passing Earth chart either side of today and read the note under it.

      Show answer

      Most small asteroids are discovered only days before or after they fly by. The future list holds only the ones already known, so it always looks emptier than it will turn out.

    4. On the Palermo scale, which known asteroids rate between −2 and 0, and what does that range mean?

      Use the lab: Read the top rows of The risk list and the explanation above it.

      Show answer

      Only 1950 DA (29075) and Bennu (101955). Between −2 and 0 means the risk merits careful monitoring; below −2 means no likely consequences.

    5. HistoryWhere is India’s crater lake made by a meteorite, and how big is it?

      Use the lab: Read Rocks that changed history.

      Show answer

      Lonar, in Buldhana district, Maharashtra: 1.8 km wide and 150 m deep, punched into Deccan basalt. It is usually dated to about 52,000 years ago.
