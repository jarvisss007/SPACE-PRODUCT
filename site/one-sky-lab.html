A what-if, worked out with real numbers

One Sky Lab

Every space agency has something the others want. NASA flew people to the Moon. China brought rock back from its far side. Japan returned dust from asteroids. Europe landed on Titan. India put an orbiter around Mars for US$73 million. What if they stopped duplicating each other and worked as one? This page adds up what is real today, then lets you assemble missions from everyone’s strengths.

The money, side by side

Proof it can work

It has happened once. Five agencies, from the United States, Russia, Europe, Japan and Canada, built the International Space Station together.

It is the longest continuous human presence in space. The station shows both sides of working together: it achieved what none of the partners had done alone, and it has been called the most expensive single object ever built.

Why it doesn’t happen more

Space hardware is also military hardware: the same rocket that carries a satellite can carry a warhead, so countries guard it. US law has barred NASA from working with China without approval from the FBI and Congress since 2011. Partnerships also have to survive wars, sanctions and elections. And working together does not change physics: Mars is still months away and rockets still need the same push. What it changes is who pays, how much gets duplicated, and how fast the best ideas spread.
