A what-if, worked out with real numbers

One Sky Lab

Every space agency has something the others want. NASA flew people to the Moon. China brought rock back from its far side. Japan returned dust from asteroids. Europe landed on Titan. India put an orbiter around Mars for US$73 million. What if they stopped duplicating each other and worked as one? This page adds up what is real today and lets you assemble missions from everyone’s strengths. Part two works out, with physics, what shared research would unlock: dishes that never lose a spacecraft, a sky full of navigation satellites, a crewed trip to Mars nearly five times lighter, and one shared Moon base instead of two.

In simple words

What if every space agency in the world worked as one team? This page shows how much faster and cheaper space could be.

Try this first: Press Voyager 2 and switch on more agencies one by one. Watch the hours when nobody can hear the spacecraft disappear.

  • One country's radio dishes lose a far-away spacecraft for part of every day as Earth turns. Dishes from many countries together never lose it.
  • A phone that listens to every country's navigation satellites sees many more of them, so it finds you faster and more accurately.
  • Today two separate Moon bases are being planned, and much of their hardware is being built twice. Sharing it would save years.

The money, side by side

Proof it can work

It has happened once. Five agencies, from the United States, Russia, Europe, Japan and Canada, built the International Space Station together.

Part two · what shared research changes

Where the big wins are

Pooling money makes space cheaper by sharing it out. Pooling research changes what is possible at all. The three benches below take real hardware from every agency and work out, with physics, what happens when nobody keeps their best idea to themselves.

Shared ears: the deep-space dishes

A spacecraft near Mars or beyond can only talk to Earth through a giant dish that can see it. Earth turns, so one country’s dishes lose it for part of every day. NASA spaced its three stations about a third of the way round the world so one is always in view. Switch on the other agencies and watch the gaps close.

Shared sky: every navigation satellite

Your phone’s location comes from navigation satellites. America runs GPS, Russia GLONASS, Europe Galileo, China BeiDou, Japan QZSS and India NavIC. A receiver that listens to all of them sees far more satellites, so it works in narrow streets and fixes faster. These are the real satellites, placed from today’s orbit data, as seen from the city you pick.

Part three · a Moon base, built together

Two plans for one Moon

Right now two groups are racing to build bases at the same place, the Moon’s south pole, where sunlit ridges sit next to craters holding ice. The US-led Artemis programme is backed by the Artemis Accords, signed by 78 countries as of October 2026, India among them since 2023. China and Russia lead the International Lunar Research Station, with at least 13 member countries. Most of what they are building, they are building twice.

Supply the base

Everything the crew uses has to be landed on the Moon, and every kilogram landed needs several more in Earth orbit to get there. Set the base up and compare two separate bases with one shared one.

What it takes

How the base model works. Getting to the Moon’s surface from low Earth orbit takes 5.93 km/s (Wikipedia “Delta-v budget”). Blue Moon burns hydrogen (450 s, tanks 13% of the fuel) and Starship burns methane (380 s, tanks 10%), both assumed. A person uses about 5 kg of food, water and oxygen a day (Wikipedia “Life-support system”). With recycling this page assumes 2 kg; with ice, only about 1 kg of food is shipped. Each reactor gives 40 kW and weighs 6 t, NASA’s older target (it now aims for 100 kW by 2030). Assumed sizing: power of 20 kW plus 5 kW per person, a 20 t habitat per four people, 15 t of landing pad, radio and lab plus a 10 t rover per base, 1 t of spares and science per person per year, and an 8 t ice plant. The bill counts launches only, not building the hardware.

For experts: How far to trust the Moon base numbers

The Mars model is a sketch. The mission stays the same throughout: a cargo lander and a crew ship, each pushed from low Earth orbit to Mars (3.6 km/s). Into orbit there costs 1.2 km/s, or a heat shield of 15% of the mass. Home costs 1.5 km/s. Landing is 0.9 km/s plus a 15% heat shield, and the ascent is 5 km/s on methane–oxygen (360 s). The surface cargo (55 t) and crew ship (55 t) were set once so the chemical case matches NASA’s 657 t. Fuel tanks weigh 8–20% of the fuel they hold. A nuclear stage carries one 18.1 t NERVA engine the whole way. Hydrogen slowly boiling away, crew radiation and launch failures are left out. The dish chart ignores mountains and assumes a 10° minimum height above the horizon. The navigation table counts every satellite in CelesTrak’s list, including a few spares and retired ones. Its “DOP” solves for one clock per system, as real receivers do.

Why it doesn’t happen more

Space hardware is also military hardware: the same rocket that carries a satellite can carry a warhead, so countries guard it. US law has barred NASA from working with China without approval from the FBI and Congress since 2011. Partnerships also have to survive wars, sanctions and elections. And working together does not change physics: Mars is still months away and rockets still need the same push. What it changes is who pays, how much gets duplicated, and how fast the best ideas spread.

Quiz: five questions

Answer each one by using the lab above, then check. Good for a class: pairs work through them, the teacher reveals the answers.

  1. The page lists two Mars orbiters from 2013: India's Mangalyaan and NASA's MAVEN. How much did each cost?

    Use the lab: Scroll to Same goal, different price and read the two Mars orbiter rows and the note under the table.

    Show answer

    Mangalyaan cost $73M and MAVEN $583M. MAVEN cost 8.0 times as much, though it carried different instruments and had a longer prime mission.

  2. If every agency puts 10% of its budget into one pool, how many Mangalyaan-class and MAVEN-class missions could it pay for each year?

    Use the lab: In Pool the money, move Share of each budget put into the pool from 5% to 10%.

    Show answer

    The joint budget becomes about $5.8 billion a year: about 79 Mangalyaan-class missions or 10 MAVEN-class missions a year (double the 5% figures of $2.9 billion, 39 and 5.0).

  3. For a spacecraft where Voyager 2 is in the sky, how much of the day can NASA's dishes alone talk to it, and what changes when everyone shares?

    Use the lab: In Shared ears: the deep-space dishes, choose NASA and the Voyager 2 position (−58°). Read In contact and Longest silence. Then click Everyone.

    Show answer

    NASA alone: 76% of each day, with a longest silence of 5.7 hours. Everyone together: 100% of the day, no silence, and two or more dishes at once all day. Dishes spread around the world always keep one in view.

  4. Why does a better engine shrink the “mountain” of mass needed in Earth orbit for a crewed Mars trip?

    Use the lab: In The research levers: people to Mars, set Engine for the trip between planets to ISRO Vikas and untick both levers. Read The mountain of mass in orbit. Then pick NASA NERVA and tick Aerocapture and Make fuel on Mars.

    Show answer

    Vikas with no tricks needs about 2,348 t in orbit. NERVA with both levers needs about 475 t, 4.9× lighter. NERVA's specific impulse (841 s against 293 s) means each kilogram of fuel gives far more push, and the levers brake in Mars's air and make fuel for the trip home on Mars, so less is carried from Earth.

  5. HistoryBringing a sample back through Earth's air has been done by only three agencies. Which first missions does the page name for them?

    Use the lab: In Assemble a mission from everyone's best, click Bring rock home from Mars and read the step Bring a sample back through Earth's air.

    Show answer

    NASA: Apollo 11, CNSA: Chang'e 5 and JAXA: Hayabusa. Each is the first mission in which that agency showed it could do this step.
