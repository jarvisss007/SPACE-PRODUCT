India’s own GPS · geometry of a position fix · four satellites or nothing

NavIC Lab

NavIC is India’s satellite navigation system, built so that India does not depend on America’s GPS. Its satellites hang high over the Indian Ocean: three stay over fixed points, and four trace figure-eights across the equator. A receiver works out where it is by timing signals from several satellites at once. That needs at least four working satellites in view: one each for east, north and height, and one more for the receiver’s own clock. In March 2026, after a string of atomic-clock failures, only three NavIC satellites could still give positions. Then on 30 October 2026 a GSLV launched NVS-03, and by 4 November it had reached its orbit, bringing NavIC back to four: the bare minimum. This page shows what three and four mean.

In simple words

NavIC is India's own version of GPS. A phone needs signals from at least four satellites to work out exactly where it is.

Try this first: Press March 2026: 3, then Now, since Nov 2026: 4, and watch what changes.

  • With only three working satellites, NavIC could not give anyone their position on its own.
  • A new satellite, NVS-03, brought it back to four: just enough.
  • More satellites, spread across the sky, give a more accurate position.

What a receiver in each city gets

Position error is roughly the error in each satellite’s range times a number called PDOP, which depends only on the satellites’ geometry. Satellites spread across the sky give a small PDOP; bunched satellites give a large one. Below 4 is good; above 10 is poor. Here are the best and worst values across a whole day.

Then and now

From wartime radio beacons to India’s own navigation satellites. Tap a year to open it; green dots mark India’s part.

  1. 1942LORAN: finding your way by radio

    During the Second World War, American engineers built LORAN (“long range navigation”). Ships timed radio pulses from pairs of stations on the coast, and the difference in arrival times told them where they were. The first chain went live in June 1942. NavIC works on the same idea, timing signals, but its stations are in space.

  2. 1960–64Transit, the first navigation satellites

    The US Navy launched Transit 1B on 13 April 1960. Ships listened to how the satellite’s radio tone shifted as it passed overhead and worked out their position from that. Transit was declared working in 1964 and was used until 1996, when GPS replaced it.

  3. 1978The first GPS satellite

    On 22 February 1978 the first GPS satellite, Navstar 1, was launched from California. GPS was built by the US military; today anyone’s phone can use it, but the United States still runs it.

  4. 1999Kargil: a lesson about depending on othersIndia

    During the Kargil conflict in 1999, it was widely reported that the United States turned down India’s request for GPS data of the area. The full story is hard to check, but it is often told as one reason India decided to build a navigation system of its own.

  5. 2006India says yes to IRNSSIndia

    In May 2006 the Government of India approved the Indian Regional Navigation Satellite System (IRNSS), later named NavIC. Instead of covering the whole world like GPS, it would cover India and about 1,500 km around it.

  6. 2013IRNSS-1A, the first satelliteIndia

    On 1 July 2013 a PSLV rocket launched IRNSS-1A from Sriharikota. More followed, making up the seven-satellite design you can switch on at the top of this page.

  7. 2017–18The atomic clocks start failingIndia

    Every navigation satellite carries atomic clocks, because a timing error of a millionth of a second means a position error of about 300 m. In January 2017 all three imported clocks on IRNSS-1A were reported to have failed. More failures followed, and by May 2018 about nine clocks across the fleet had stopped. A satellite with no working clock can still send messages but cannot help you find your position.

  8. 2017IRNSS-1H is lost at launchIndia

    IRNSS-1H was meant to replace the clock-less IRNSS-1A. It launched on a PSLV on 31 August 2017, but the heat shield, the nose cover that protects the satellite on the way up, did not come off. Trapped inside, the satellite could not separate and never reached its orbit.

  9. 2020NavIC arrives in phonesIndia

    In January 2020 the chip-maker Qualcomm released phone chips that could pick up NavIC signals, and some phones able to use NavIC went on sale that year. Most such phones combine NavIC with GPS and other systems.

  10. 2023NVS-01 brings an Indian clockIndia

    On 29 May 2023 a GSLV launched NVS-01, the first of NavIC’s second-generation satellites. For the first time it carried a rubidium atomic clock made in India, at ISRO’s Space Applications Centre in Ahmedabad.

  11. 2025NVS-02 gets stuckIndia

    NVS-02 was launched on 29 January 2025. The rocket did its job, but in early February the satellite’s own engine could not fire because valves on its fuel lines did not open. So it never climbed to its proper orbit and is stuck in a stretched loop.

  12. 2026NVS-03: back to fourIndia

    By March 2026 only three NavIC satellites could still give positions, one short of the four needed. On 30 October 2026 a GSLV launched NVS-03, and by 4 November it had reached its orbit, bringing NavIC back to four: the bare minimum this page shows.

For experts: Checks
For experts: What the model leaves out

Orbits are perfect circles at geosynchronous height, using the published slots: geostationary at 32.5°E, 83°E and 129.5°E, inclined about 5°, and two inclined planes crossing the equator at 55°E and 111.75°E at 29°. The phasing of the two satellites sharing each inclined plane is not published in our sources, so it is assumed to be a quarter of a day apart. (Half a day would put both at the figure-eight’s crossing point at the same moment, twice a day, collapsing the geometry.) When each slot starts its daily loop is a guess too, so the exact time of the worst moment may differ. Signals are counted from 5° above the horizon, and buildings, terrain and the ionosphere are ignored. The map shows geometry only; it is not a forecast of service. NavIC can also use GPS satellites alongside its own in combined receivers, and this page leaves that out.

Quiz: five questions

Answer each one by using the lab above, then check. Good for a class: pairs work through them, the teacher reveals the answers.

  1. What is the smallest number of working satellites a receiver needs to find its position? Why that many?

    Use the lab: Read the introduction at the top of the page. Then click March 2026: 3 and read the message under the map.

    Show answer

    4 satellites. The receiver has 4 unknowns: east, north, height, and the error in its own clock. Each satellite gives one equation, so 3 satellites give no fix at all.

  2. How good is a position in Delhi with all 7 satellites working as designed?

    Use the lab: Click As designed: 7. In the table What a receiver in each city gets, read the Delhi row.

    Show answer

    Delhi sees 7 satellites. PDOP stays between about 3.1 and 3.4 all day, so the error is about 15–17 m at 5 m per range. Below 4 counts as good.

  3. With the full 7-satellite design, compare Delhi with Perth in Australia. Why is Perth so much worse?

    Use the lab: Keep As designed: 7 selected. In the city table, compare the Worst PDOP for Delhi and Perth. Then click Show the worst moment of the day and look at the map.

    Show answer

    Delhi’s worst PDOP is about 3.4; Perth’s is about 31, with only 4–6 satellites in view. NavIC’s satellites hang over the Indian Ocean to serve India. Perth is far from them, so they sit low and bunched in its sky, and the geometry is poor.

  4. Since NVS-03 arrived in November 2026, NavIC has 4 working satellites. Is that enough for a safe service? Why or why not?

    Use the lab: Click Now, since Nov 2026: 4. Read the message under the map and the Delhi row of the city table.

    Show answer

    It gives a fix, but a weak one. In this model Delhi’s PDOP ranges from about 6.5 to over 1,000 during the day. With exactly four, losing any one satellite, or one dipping behind a hill, ends the fix. There is no spare until NVS-04 and NVS-05 are launched.

  5. HistoryWhy did India build NavIC, and what happened to it in 2026?

    Use the lab: Read the introduction, then the satellite list on the right (the coloured status tags).

    Show answer

    India built NavIC so it would not depend on America’s GPS. After a string of atomic-clock failures, by March 2026 only 3 satellites could still give positions: IRNSS-1B, IRNSS-1I and NVS-01, which replaced IRNSS-1G. NVS-03, launched on 30 October 2026, brought it back to the minimum of 4.
