Aditya-L1 · the three-body problem · Lagrange 1772

Lagrange Point Lab

India’s solar observatory Aditya-L1 sits 1.5 million km from Earth, towards the Sun, where it never passes into Earth’s shadow and watches the Sun all day, every day. It is not orbiting Earth, and it is not quite orbiting the Sun the way Earth does. It holds station at one of the five points Joseph-Louis Lagrange found in 1772, where the pulls of the Sun and Earth and the swing of the orbit balance out. This page finds those points and computes the orbit Aditya-L1 flies around one of them.

In simple words

There are five special places in space where a spacecraft can stay in step with Earth as both go round the Sun.

Try this first: Press L1 (Aditya-L1, SOHO) to see where India's Sun observatory sits, then L2 (James Webb).

  • At these places the pull of the Sun and Earth together keeps a spacecraft moving in step with Earth.
  • L1 sits between Earth and the Sun, so it can watch the Sun all the time.
  • L2 sits on the far side of Earth from the Sun, which helps the James Webb telescope stay cold.

The five balance points

The view turns with Earth, so the Sun and Earth stay still. The shading is the “landscape” a spacecraft feels in this turning frame, combining gravity with the outward swing of the turn. The five points are where that landscape is flat. In the near view, distances from Earth are true.

Where they are

Found by solving for where the forces cancel, using only the masses of the Sun, Earth and Moon. L4 and L5 sit at the corners of equilateral triangles with the Sun and Earth.

Give it a nudge

Put a spacecraft exactly on a point and it stays. But nothing is ever exact. Here it starts a few hundred kilometres off and drifts with nothing but gravity. Watch which points hold on to it.

Aditya-L1’s halo orbit

Because L1 will not hold a spacecraft still, Aditya-L1 circles it instead in a “halo orbit”, a loop about 1.3 million km long that is tilted so the spacecraft never sits right in front of the Sun as seen from Earth, where the Sun’s radio noise would drown out its signal. There is no formula for a halo orbit. Here it is found the way mission designers do it: guess, fly it for half a lap, measure the miss, correct the starting point, and repeat until the orbit closes on itself.

Drag to turn it. The Sun is far off to the left; Earth is to the right.

Then and now

From a maths puzzle to a busy neighbourhood of spacecraft. Tap a year to open it. The orange dot marks India’s part.

  1. 1760sEuler finds three balance points

    Leonhard Euler showed that three bodies lined up in a row can keep their pattern as they move. That gives the three points on the Sun–Earth line, now called L1, L2 and L3; his paper was printed in 1767.

  2. 1772Lagrange finds all five

    Joseph-Louis Lagrange, in his essay on the three-body problem, found Euler’s three points again plus two more, L4 and L5. Each sits at the tip of an equal-sided triangle with the two big bodies.

  3. 1906Nature got there first

    Max Wolf in Germany discovered the asteroid 588 Achilles, sharing Jupiter’s orbit at one of its triangle points. It was the first “Trojan”; thousands are known today.

  4. 1978ISEE-3, the first spacecraft at L1

    NASA’s ISEE-3 reached a halo orbit around L1 on 20 November 1978, the first spacecraft ever to fly one. In 1985 it left and flew through the tail of comet Giacobini-Zinner.

  5. 1995SOHO moves in at L1

    Europe and NASA’s SOHO launched in December 1995 to watch the Sun from L1. Planned for two years, it is still working after 30.

  6. 2001WMAP goes to L2

    NASA’s WMAP reached L2, on the far side of Earth, on 1 October 2001. Mapping the faint heat left from the Big Bang, it worked out the universe is about 13.8 billion years old.

  7. 2013–2025Gaia maps the Milky Way

    Europe’s Gaia launched on 19 December 2013 and measured about 2 billion stars from L2. It was switched off and sent away from L2 on 27 March 2025.

  8. 2021–2022James Webb arrives at L2

    The James Webb Space Telescope launched on 25 December 2021 and reached its halo orbit around L2 on 24 January 2022. L2 keeps the Sun, Earth and Moon all on one side, behind its sunshield, so it stays cold.

  9. 6 Jan 2024Aditya-L1 reaches L1 India

    ISRO’s Aditya-L1, launched on 2 September 2023, was placed in its halo orbit around L1 on 6 January 2024. It finished its first lap, about 178 days, on 2 July 2024. It is India’s first mission at a Lagrange point.

  10. 2025–2026More company at L1

    NASA’s IMAP and NOAA’s space-weather satellite SWFO-L1 launched together on 24 September 2025. IMAP reached L1 on 10 January 2026, and SWFO-L1 followed later that month.

  11. Now, 2026Roman heads for L2

    NASA’s Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope launched on 30 August 2026 on a three-month trip to L2. It is due to arrive around the end of 2026.

For experts: Checks against the real mission
For experts: What the model leaves out

This is the circular restricted three-body problem: the Sun and the Earth–Moon pair move in perfect circles, and the spacecraft is too small to pull on them. Earth’s orbit is really slightly oval, the Moon tugs the spacecraft each month, and sunlight presses on it. Those are the pushes Aditya-L1’s station-keeping burns correct. In the nudge test L1 and L2 start 300 km off; L4 starts 100,000 km off and is flown for 500 years, because its loop takes centuries.

Quiz: five questions

Answer each one by using the lab above, then check. Good for a class: pairs work through them, the teacher reveals the answers.

  1. How far from Earth is the L1 point, where Aditya-L1 works?

    Use the lab: Read the Where they are table, row L1.

    Show answer

    About 1,497,621 km, roughly 1.5 million km towards the Sun. That matches ISRO’s “about 1.5 million km”.

  2. Which loop height gives a halo orbit that takes exactly as long as Aditya-L1’s real one?

    Use the lab: In Aditya-L1’s halo orbit, move the slider How far the loop rises above Earth’s orbit plane step by step and watch Time for one lap.

    Show answer

    About 120,000 km out of the plane gives 177.86 days, ISRO’s figure for Aditya-L1. The whole family only ranges from about 177.59 to 177.89 days, so the lap time hardly changes with size.

  3. Let a spacecraft go near L1, then near L4. Which point holds on to it?

    Use the lab: In Give it a nudge, click L1 (Aditya-L1, SOHO) and read the results. Then click L4 (Trojan asteroids at Jupiter) and compare.

    Show answer

    From just 300 km off L1, it is more than 1 million km away after about 190 days: it slides off. From 100,000 km off L4 it wanders up to about 81 million km but never escapes, looping back about every 230 years.

  4. Why does Aditya-L1 have to fire its thrusters a few times every year?

    Use the lab: Click L1 (Aditya-L1, SOHO) in Give it a nudge and read the message. Then read the row Why station-keeping is needed in the checks table.

    Show answer

    L1 is like the top of a saddle. A small error grows by about 2.7 times every 23 days, about 8 million times in a year if left alone. Small, regular burns (0.2 to 4 m/s a year) keep it on its halo orbit.

  5. HistoryWho found the five balance points, and when? Who later worked out halo orbits?

    Use the lab: Read the introduction at the top of the page and the Method line in the footer.

    Show answer

    Joseph-Louis Lagrange, in 1772. Halo orbits came much later, from R. W. Farquhar (1968) and K. C. Howell (1984). Aditya-L1 launched on 2 September 2023 and arrived on 6 January 2024.
