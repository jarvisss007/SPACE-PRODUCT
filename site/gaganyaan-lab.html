Gaganyaan Lab

Gaganyaan (“sky craft” in Sanskrit) is the capsule ISRO is building to fly Indian astronauts on an Indian rocket. Before anyone rides it, ISRO has to prove three things: the crew can escape if the rocket fails, the ship can live in orbit, and the capsule can come home through the fire and land softly in the sea. This lab lets you fly all three, with a model checked against ISRO’s real test flights.

In simple words

Gaganyaan is the capsule India is building to fly its own astronauts. Here you can test its escape system, its orbit and its return home.

Try this first: Press TV-D1, 2023 to replay ISRO's real test where the escape rockets pulled the capsule away from a rocket.

  • If the rocket fails, small powerful rockets on top yank the crew capsule away in seconds.
  • In orbit, the capsule circles Earth about every 90 minutes, 400 km up.
  • Coming home, the air slows it down from very fast, then parachutes bring it gently into the sea.

1Escape!

On top of the capsule sits the Crew Escape System: solid rocket motors that can yank the crew module off a failing rocket in a fraction of a second. ISRO has tested it twice. Replay those tests, or pick your own moment for the rocket to fail.

2In orbit

Once in orbit, the crew circles Earth at about 400 km. Move the height to see how the orbit changes. Lower is cheaper to reach but the thin air there drags the ship down sooner.

Earth and the orbit, to scale

3Coming home

To come home, the service module fires its five 440-newton engines against the direction of travel. That small push lowers the far side of the orbit into the air. Too gentle and the capsule only grazes the atmosphere. Too hard and it dives in steeply, the braking crushes the crew and the heat shield takes a beating.

    For experts: How far to trust it

    The escape motor’s push and the parachute sizes are not published. They were set once so the model matches the height and distance of the 2018 pad abort and lands under 11 m/s. Everything about TV-D1 below is then a real prediction, worked out by the page each time it loads.

    For experts: What the model leaves out

    Flights are worked out in one vertical plane with no wind, through the US Standard Atmosphere 1976. The escape is a single steady thrust of 975 kN for 5.98 s on a 12.6 t stack, steered by a steady pitch-over. The real system has five kinds of motor, grid fins and its own sequence. The rocket in the escape chart is simply shut down and left to coast, as TV-D1’s was. The return uses the Re-entry Lab model: constant drag and lift, the Sutton–Graves heating estimate, a round Earth that does not spin. Parachutes open on a fixed rule: drogues once the capsule has slowed below 216 m/s, mains at 2 km. Reefing holds each main at 15% of its area for the first 7 s, as in the 2022 drop test. Masses: 12.6 t for the escape stack and 4.5 t for the crew module, from the two tests. The service module’s engine efficiency (315 s) is assumed.

    Quiz: five questions

    Answer each one by using the lab above, then check. Good for a class: pairs work through them, the teacher reveals the answers.

    1. How long does Gaganyaan take to go once around the Earth at its planned orbit height?

      Use the lab: In section 2 In orbit, set the Orbit height slider to 400 km and read the Once around tile.

      Show answer

      About 92 min (92.4 min). At 400 km the ship moves at about 27,600 km/h, so one lap of the Earth takes about an hour and a half.

    2. Compare the two real escape tests. How high did the crew module climb in each one?

      Use the lab: In section 1 Escape!, click Pad abort, 2018 and read Top of the arc. Then click TV-D1, 2023 and read it again.

      Show answer

      Pad abort: about 2.79 km. TV-D1: about 16.5 km. In TV-D1 the rocket was already about 11 km up and moving at Mach 1.2, so the capsule kept that speed and climbed much higher.

    3. What happens to the splashdown speed in the TV-D1 escape if one main parachute fails to open? Is the crew still safe?

      Use the lab: Click TV-D1, 2023. Read Splashdown speed. Then tick One main parachute fails to open and read it again.

      Show answer

      It rises from about 7.5 m/s to about 10.6 m/s. That is still under the 11 m/s limit, so the crew is safe. One chute is enough, and the second one is a spare for safety.

    4. Why does the autopilot matter when coming home? Try a hard braking burn with and without it.

      Use the lab: In section 3 Coming home, set Braking burn to 400 m/s. Read Hardest braking with Autopilot: roll the lift to stay under 4 g. Then change Steering to No lift (ballistic backup) and read it again.

      Show answer

      With the autopilot about 7.9 g. With no lift about 15.6 g, which the page marks Too steep for the crew (over 10 g). The capsule's lift lets it slow down more gently over a longer time, instead of diving straight into thick air.

    5. HistoryWho was the first Indian in space, and in which year? Which spacecraft did he fly on?

      Use the lab: Scroll to The road so far and read the first entry in the timeline.

      Show answer

      Rakesh Sharma, in 1984. He flew on the Soviet Soyuz T-11 under the Interkosmos programme. Gaganyaan aims to put the first Indian crew on an Indian rocket.
