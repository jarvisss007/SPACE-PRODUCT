Launch Watch

Since Sputnik on 4 October 1957, humanity has launched thousands of rockets towards orbit. In 2026 there is one somewhere on Earth every day or so: from Florida and California, China’s four spaceports, New Zealand, Kazakhstan, French Guiana, Japan, and Sriharikota on India’s east coast. The world’s spaceports

Circle size: launches from that site this year. Orange rings: sites with a launch in the coming list. Notice how many sit on an east coast, or with open sea to the east: rockets fly east to borrow Earth’s spin, and spent stages must fall where no one lives.

Why near the equator?

Earth spins east, so a rocket launched east starts with a free push. At the equator the ground moves at 465 m/s, and the push shrinks as you go towards the poles. Latitude matters even more for satellites going to geostationary orbit, which must end up exactly over the equator: a rocket cannot easily reach an orbit tilted less than its launch site’s latitude, and untilting it later costs fuel.

The price of latitude

Solid line: the extra push needed at the top of a standard transfer orbit to circle over the equator, if the orbit starts tilted by the site’s latitude. Dashed: the free push from Earth’s spin.

For experts: How far to trust it
For experts: What the page leaves out

Launch times are “no earlier than” targets and slip often. The coming and recent launches load live from Launch Library 2, and your browser keeps a copy for 30 minutes because the free service allows only 15 requests an hour. The year’s totals come from a copy refreshed once a day. Countries here are where the launch site is, not who built the rocket or the satellite. The latitude chart assumes a launch due east, a standard transfer orbit with a high point at geostationary height and a low point near 200 km, and the whole tilt removed in one burn at the high point. Real launches often fly other paths for safety, so their orbits can start more tilted than the site’s latitude.

Quiz: five questions

Answer each one by using the lab above, then check. Good for a class: pairs work through them, the teacher reveals the answers.

  1. How much free push does a rocket get from Earth’s spin when it launches due east from Sriharikota, and from the equator?

    Use the lab: Click Sriharikota under Why near the equator? and read Free push from Earth’s spin. Then click Kourou.

    Show answer

    About 452 m/s from Sriharikota (13.7° N) and 463 m/s from Kourou (5° N). At the equator itself it is 465 m/s, the speed the ground moves as Earth spins.

  2. Why does a satellite launched from Baikonur need a bigger push to reach geostationary orbit than one launched from Kourou?

    Use the lab: Compare Extra over a launch from the equator for Baikonur and Kourou, and look at the orange curve.

    Show answer

    A rocket cannot easily reach an orbit tilted less than its site’s latitude. From Baikonur (46°) the orbit starts tilted 46°, and untilting it at the top of the transfer orbit costs several hundred m/s more than from Kourou (5°).

  3. Which country launched the most rockets this year, and how many launches were there in all?

    Use the lab: Read This year so far and the country chart.

    Show answer

    The United States, then China. The totals are refreshed daily, so read the current numbers on the page (274 launches by 18 November 2026, about one every 28 hours).

  4. Which Indian rocket that is not ISRO’s flew from Sriharikota in 2026?

    Use the lab: Read the India this year table.

    Show answer

    Vikram-I, built by the private company Skyroot Aerospace, on a successful demonstration flight in July 2026.

  5. HistoryWhen did the first satellite reach orbit, and what was it?

    Use the lab: Read the introduction at the top of the page.

    Show answer

    Sputnik 1, launched by the Soviet Union on 4 October 1957.
