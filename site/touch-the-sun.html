Parker Solar Probe · seven Venus flybys · 191 km/s

Touch the Sun

The Sun is the hardest place in the solar system to reach. Earth carries everything launched from it around the Sun at 29.8 km/s, and to fall inward a spacecraft has to cancel almost all of that sideways speed. Leaving the solar system altogether takes less. NASA’s Parker Solar Probe got there by passing Venus seven times, each pass shedding a little more speed. On 24 December 2024 it skimmed 6.16 million km above the Sun’s surface at 191 km/s, the fastest anything people have built has ever gone.

In simple words

Reaching the Sun is surprisingly hard: you have to cancel Earth's sideways speed, not just fly towards it.

Try this first: Press Play the seven years to watch the Parker Solar Probe get closer and closer.

  • Earth races around the Sun at about 30 km every second. To fall towards the Sun, a spacecraft must lose most of that speed.
  • Parker used Venus's gravity seven times to slow down and fall closer.
  • At its closest, Parker is the fastest object people have ever made.

The test: rebuild the orbit table

NASA published each orbit’s closest distance, period and top speed. Kepler and Newton connect the three: from the distance and the period alone, the top speed follows. Then, for each Venus flyby, the bend between one published orbit and the next fixes how close Parker had to pass Venus. That is compared with the mission plan.

The flyby altitudes were planned in 2014, before launch, and the real flybys were adjusted afterwards, so the comparison there is looser. In every case the speed relative to Venus stays the same before and after, about 23.3 km/s, as a flyby with no engine firing requires.

Then and now

From the first sunspot records to today. Tap a year to open it. Orange dots mark India’s part.

  1. 28 BCSpots on the Sun, written down

    Astronomers in Han-dynasty China wrote down a dark spot seen on the Sun, the oldest dated sunspot record. They saw it without any telescope, when haze dimmed the Sun.

  2. about 1610Telescopes find sunspots

    Galileo, Harriot, Fabricius and Scheiner turned the new telescopes on the Sun and watched sunspots move. That showed the Sun spins.

  3. 1899–1909Kodaikanal watches the Sun India

    India’s Kodaikanal Solar Observatory opened in 1899 in the hills of Tamil Nadu and has photographed the Sun ever since. In 1909 John Evershed found there that gas flows outward across sunspots, now called the Evershed effect.

  4. 1976Helios 2 sets the record

    Germany and the US sent Helios 2 to within about 43 million km of the Sun, at about 70 km/s. Nothing went closer or faster for 42 years.

  5. 1990–1994Ulysses flies over the poles

    Europe and NASA launched Ulysses in 1990. Jupiter’s gravity threw it out of the planets’ flat plane, and in 1994 it became the first spacecraft to pass over the Sun’s south pole.

  6. 1995SOHO starts watching

    Europe and NASA’s SOHO launched in December 1995 to the L1 point, between Earth and the Sun. Planned for two years, it is still working after 30, and has found more than 5,000 comets.

  7. 2018–2021Parker launches, then enters the corona

    NASA’s Parker Solar Probe launched on 12 August 2018. On 28 April 2021 it flew inside the Sun’s outer atmosphere, the corona, for the first time: “touching the Sun”.

  8. 2020–2025Solar Orbiter sees a pole

    Europe’s Solar Orbiter launched on 10 February 2020. Venus tilted its orbit, and in June 2025 it showed the first pictures ever of the Sun’s south pole.

  9. 2023–2024Aditya-L1, India’s Sun observatory India

    ISRO launched Aditya-L1 on 2 September 2023, and it reached L1, 1.5 million km away, on 6 January 2024. From there it watches the Sun all day, every day, and ISRO began releasing its data to everyone in January 2025.

  10. Dec 2024Closest ever: 6.16 million km

    On 24 December 2024 Parker passed 6.16 million km above the Sun’s surface at 191 km/s. That is the closest and fastest any spacecraft has ever been.

  11. Now, 2026Still diving in every 88 days

    Parker repeats that record pass every 88 days; its 29th close pass was on 4 September 2026, and NASA has extended the mission into 2029. Solar Orbiter’s next Venus flyby, planned for 24 December 2026, tilts it further to see the poles better.

For experts: What the model leaves out

All orbits are drawn with their closest points lined up and in one plane; in reality each flyby also turned the orbit a little. Venus’s orbit is a circle. The first flyby is left out of the test because it depends on the launch orbit, which our sources do not give. The sunlight figure is for a surface facing the Sun squarely; how hot the shield gets also depends on its coating and how it sheds heat.

Quiz: five questions

Answer each one by using the lab above, then check. Good for a class: pairs work through them, the teacher reveals the answers.

  1. After the seventh Venus flyby, how long does Parker take to go once around the Sun, and what is its published top speed?

    Use the lab: Drag the After Venus flyby slider to 7 and read Lap time and Top speed, published in the This orbit box.

    Show answer

    88 days and 192 km/s. Each flyby shrank the orbit, so the lap got shorter and the top speed got higher.

  2. Which needs a bigger push from Earth's orbit: leaving the solar system for ever, or hitting the Sun's surface?

    Use the lab: Look at the bar chart in Why the Sun is so hard to reach. Compare the bars Leave the solar system for ever and Hit the Sun's surface.

    Show answer

    Hitting the Sun needs about 26.9 km/s, more than twice the 12.3 km/s needed to leave the solar system. To fall into the Sun you must cancel almost all of Earth's 29.8 km/s sideways speed.

  3. How much stronger is sunlight at closest approach after flyby 1 compared with after flyby 7?

    Use the lab: Set the After Venus flyby slider to 1 and read the sunlight line under This orbit. Then move it to 7 and read it again.

    Show answer

    About 36 times Earth's sunlight after flyby 1, and about 476 times after flyby 7 (NASA quotes 475). Sunlight grows as one over distance squared, and the closest distance fell from 24.81 to 6.86 million km.

  4. Parker's rocket gave it only about 12.7 km/s. Why could it not fly straight to its final orbit at 9.86 solar radii?

    Use the lab: In the bar chart, compare What Parker's rocket gave it, worked out from its first orbit with Reach Parker's final 9.86 solar radii, directly. Then press Play the seven years to watch the orbit shrink.

    Show answer

    Going there directly needs about 21.0 km/s, but the rocket gave only 12.7 km/s. So Parker used seven Venus passes, each one shedding a little more speed, to shrink the orbit step by step.

  5. HistoryThe header says that on 24 December 2024 Parker passed 6.16 million km above the Sun's surface. The lab shows a different closest distance. Why do the two numbers differ?

    Use the lab: Set the After Venus flyby slider to 7 and read Closest to the Sun's centre. Compare it with the header text and the solar radius in the footer.

    Show answer

    The lab shows 6.86 million km from the Sun's centre. Subtract the solar radius, 695,700 km (about 0.70 million km), and you get about 6.16 million km above the surface. That 2024 pass, at 191 km/s, is the fastest anything people have built has gone.
