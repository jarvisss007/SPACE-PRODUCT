Shooting stars · comet dust · your own sky

Meteor Showers

A shooting star is a grain of dust, often smaller than a grain of sand, hitting the air at up to 70 km/s and burning up 100 km above you. Most of that dust was shed by comets. Their crumbs spread along the comet’s path, and every year Earth ploughs through the same streams on the same dates. Pick a shower to see when and where to look from your city, how the Moon will interfere, and why it happens on that date at all.

In simple words

Shooting stars are tiny bits of comet dust burning up in the air. On the same nights every year, Earth passes through the same dust.

Try this first: Choose your City and how dark Your sky is. The page shows the best hour to watch.

  • The best showers give up to about 100 shooting stars an hour from a really dark place, far fewer in a city.
  • Watch after midnight, lie back and look at the whole sky. A bright Moon hides the faint ones.
  • Each shower comes from one comet, which left its dust along its path.

Why on this date?

Each shower belongs to a parent: a comet, or in two cases a rocky asteroid. Its dust spreads along the parent’s orbit. Where that orbit passes closest to Earth’s, Earth runs into the dust, at the same point in its orbit every year. This chart works the date out from the parent’s orbit alone and checks it against the date observers see.

A grain with a bullet’s punch

Speed is everything. Energy of motion grows with the square of speed, so a one-gram grain at shower speeds carries more energy than the same mass of explosive. That energy heats the grain and the air around it until both glow.

Then and now

From “stars fell like rain” to radar and sky cameras. Tap a year to open it; green dots mark India’s part.

  1. 687 BCEStars fall like rain over China

    The Chinese chronicle called the Zuo Zhuan says that one night that year, at midnight, “stars fell like rain”. It was the Lyrids, and it is one of the oldest meteor shower records anywhere.

  2. 1833The great Leonid storm

    On the night of 12–13 November 1833 the sky over North America filled with meteors: by one estimate more than 100,000 an hour. Denison Olmsted collected reports, saw that they all flew from the direction of Leo, and suggested they came from a cloud of particles out in space. Until then most people thought meteors were a kind of weather. Meteor science was born that night.

  3. 1866Schiaparelli finds the parent

    Comet Swift–Tuttle swept past the Sun in 1862. In 1866 the Italian astronomer Giovanni Schiaparelli showed that the Perseids travel along the same path as that comet. It was the first proof that meteor showers are comet dust: the idea this whole page is built on.

  4. 1946–47Meteors on radar

    During the Draconid storm of 9–10 October 1946, Bernard Lovell’s radar at Jodrell Bank in England picked up echoes from meteor trails. When the aerial was turned away from the shower, the echoes nearly stopped: proof they were meteors. In May 1947 his team left the radar on after sunrise and found meteors still arriving: showers that happen in daylight, which no eye can see.

  5. 1959Cameras catch a meteorite

    On 7 April 1959 cameras run by the Ondřejov Observatory in Czechoslovakia photographed a brilliant fireball from more than one place. From the pictures, astronomers worked out its path, found pieces of the Příbram meteorite on the ground, and worked out the orbit the rock had followed around the Sun. It was the first time ever, and it led to the European Fireball Network that still watches the sky.

  6. 1966The Leonids storm again

    On 17 November 1966 observers at Kitt Peak in Arizona saw about 40 meteors a second: roughly 144,000 an hour, far more than a person can count. Earth had run into a thick, fresh trail of dust from Comet Tempel–Tuttle.

  7. 1999An Indian radar watches the LeonidsIndia

    The Leonids gave a strong show again in 1999. From 16 to 20 November, scientists ran the giant MST radar at Gadanki, near Tirupati in Andhra Pradesh, in “meteor mode” through the night. It recorded large numbers of meteor trails and used them to measure the temperature and winds high in the air, where meteors burn up.

  8. 2010–22Meteor cameras in IndiaIndia

    In 2010 an Indian team filmed the Geminids with a portable video camera near Asangaon in Maharashtra, and noted that India had no meteor camera network of its own. By 2022, students at IISER Tirupati and IIT Madras were setting up stations for CAMS India, part of a worldwide network of about 600 cameras that triangulate each meteor to find where it came from.

  9. 2025A surprise from the Ursids

    On 22 December 2025 radio observers in 12 countries caught a small, short outburst of the Ursids, peaking at about 08:30 UT. Earth had probably crossed a thin filament of dust from Comet Tuttle. Experts had suggested something like it might happen, though a little earlier.

  10. 2026A dark-sky Perseids, a quiet year

    The Moon was new on 12 August 2026, the same day as the total solar eclipse over Spain, so the Perseids peaked that night in a moonless sky. NASA’s forecasters expected no outbursts from any shower in 2026, and the Leonids on 17 November were expected to give only about 15 an hour. Next up: the Geminids on 14 December.

For experts: How far to trust it

Every predicted date below comes only from the parent’s published orbit: the page finds where it passes closest to Earth’s orbit and when Earth reaches that spot. No shower dates are fed in.

For experts: What the model leaves out

Rates use the standard zenithal hourly rate formula: the number seen equals ZHR × sin(radiant height) ÷ r6.5 − limiting magnitude. The page assumes r = 2 for every shower; real values run about 2 to 3. The Moon is assumed to brighten the sky by up to 1.5 magnitudes when full and above the horizon. Hours count only once the Sun is 12° below the horizon. Real showers rise and fall over several nights around the peak, and a ZHR is an ideal that few observers reach. Parent orbits are today’s. Dust released centuries ago has drifted, so a few showers peak a few days from the predicted point, and Halley’s two showers come from dust far from its present path. Sun and Moon positions use the same low-precision formulas as Tonight’s Sky.

Quiz: five questions

Answer each one by using the lab above, then check. Good for a class: pairs work through them, the teacher reveals the answers.

  1. Which shower has the fastest meteors, and which comet is its parent?

    Use the lab: Look at the speeds on the shower buttons in The next showers, or at the top bar of the energy chart in A grain with a bullet's punch.

    Show answer

    The Leonids, at 70.5 km/s, from Comet 55P/Tempel–Tuttle.

  2. A Leonid is about 3.5 times faster than a Draconid. How much more energy does a 1 g Leonid grain carry?

    Use the lab: Read the bars for Leonids and Draconids in the A grain with a bullet's punch chart.

    Show answer

    Leonid: 2.49 MJ (like 594 g of TNT). Draconid: 0.20 MJ (48 g of TNT). That is about 12 times more, because energy grows with the square of speed: 3.5² ≈ 12.

  3. The page says it predicts each shower's date from the parent's orbit alone. How close is its prediction for the Perseids?

    Use the lab: Click the Perseids button. Read Closest to Earth's orbit, Predicted date, Observed peak and Difference under Why on this date?

    Show answer

    Comet Swift–Tuttle's orbit passes only 0.013 AU from Earth's. Predicted 12 Aug, observed 13 Aug: about 1 day off. Earth reaches that same point in its orbit on the same date every year.

  4. Why does Halley's Comet give us two showers a year, the Eta Aquariids in May and the Orionids in October?

    Use the lab: Click Eta Aquariids, then Orionids. Read the parent and the note under Why on this date? for each, and look at the orbit picture.

    Show answer

    Both come from Halley's Comet. Its dust is spread along its whole orbit, and Earth passes near that orbit at two places: once in May and once in October, on the other side of the orbit.

  5. HistoryWhich shower has one of the oldest records, and in which year was it recorded?

    Use the lab: Click the Lyrids button and read the note under Why on this date?

    Show answer

    The Lyrids were recorded in China in 687 BCE, among the oldest shower records. Their parent is Comet Thatcher (C/1861 G1).
