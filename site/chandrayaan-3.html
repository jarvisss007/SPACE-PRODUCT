ISRO’s own numbers · Newton’s physics · 40 days to the Moon

Chandrayaan-3 Moon Lab

On 23 August 2023 India became the fourth country to land softly on the Moon, and the first near its south pole. Apollo 11 got there in three days; Chandrayaan-3 took forty. Here the whole route is flown again from the orbits ISRO published after every engine burn, with the Moon pulling on the spacecraft all the way. Then you fly the last 30 km down yourself, with the same four 800-newton engines Vikram had.

The long way round

Every burn

Each row is an orbit ISRO announced. The simulator fires the engine at the next low point of the orbit on that date, sized to reach exactly that orbit, and lets Earth and the Moon do the rest.

Did the replay find the Moon?

The orbits come from ISRO. The Moon’s position comes from its real orbit that month. The one thing we had to fit is which way the first orbit’s long axis pointed, because our model is flat and the real orbit was tilted. We tuned that single angle until the spacecraft passed the Moon at 164 km, the low point ISRO reported. Everything below is what the physics then produced on its own.

The push budget

The speed each burn had to add or remove, worked out from ISRO’s before-and-after orbits. Around the Moon the numbers are braking: each burn lowers the far side of the orbit until Vikram is in a 25 by 134 km orbit, ready to land.

Why forty days, not three?

So the long way costs no extra push. What it saves is a bigger rocket. Apollo’s Saturn V had a third stage that fired once and threw the ship straight at the Moon. LVM3 cannot throw 3.9 tonnes that hard; it can only leave it in a stretched orbit that reaches 36,500 km. From there the spacecraft’s own small engine has to do the rest, and a small engine cannot do it in one go.

A burn only works well near the low point of the orbit, where the spacecraft moves fastest, and a small engine needs a long time to add a lot of speed: ISRO’s Moon-capture burn alone lasted 1,835 seconds. So the work is split across five low points, each one checked before the next, and the last one is timed so the Moon is waiting at the far end. Try the trade-off yourself in the Rocket Lab, where LVM3 falls well short of the push the Moon needs.

Land Vikram yourself

The real descent began at 30 km above the Moon at about 1.69 km/s, sideways. Four 800-newton engines, which cannot throttle below 40%, had to kill all of that in about 20 minutes, on a lander weighing 1.75 tonnes. At the start the engines lift only 1.1 times the lander’s weight on the Moon. In 2019, Chandrayaan-2’s lander was still falling at 58 m/s at 330 m and crashed.

What this model leaves out

The route is flown in one flat plane; the real orbits were tilted and the lunar orbit ran over the poles. The Sun’s pull is left out, and every burn happens in an instant. In the model the Moon slowly drags the orbit’s low point down by about 50 km over two weeks; ISRO’s published low points instead crept upward, from 170 to 288 km, as they trimmed the orbit. For the lander, ISRO publishes the total mass and the engines but not the propellant load or the engines’ efficiency. We assume 950 kg of propellant and a specific impulse of 310 s, typical for this type of engine. Chandrayaan-2’s lighter lander carried 845 kg.
