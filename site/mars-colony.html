Mars Colony Simulator

You are in charge. Get a crew and their whole kit off Earth, fill up with fuel in orbit, try everything out on the Moon first, fly to Mars when the planets line up, land, and then keep a small town alive on a cold, dusty planet, sol by sol, by making air, water, food and rocket fuel from what is already there. Every number comes from real missions and published studies; where a number is only a game guess, the page says so. The idea was inspired by India's Mangalyaan planners and by the film The Martian (2015), but the game is our own.

In simple words

Run a whole mission yourself: launch a crew and their kit from Earth, refuel in orbit, test everything on the Moon, fly to Mars when the planets line up, land, and then keep a small colony alive day by day by making air, water, food and fuel from Mars itself. The numbers come from real missions.

Try this first: In Stage 1, pick Starship-class and Crew of 4, then press Next stage. In Stage 2 press Dock the next tanker until the tanks are full (or press Auto-fill). Play on to Stage 6, press Play, and build an Oxygen plant first.

  • Rockets are mostly fuel: only a few tonnes in every hundred reach orbit. So Mars ships go up with empty tanks and are filled by tanker flights.
  • Earth and Mars line up for the cheap trip only about every 26 months, and the journey takes about 8 to 9 months. India's Mangalyaan cruised for 298 days.
  • Each person breathes about 0.84 kg of oxygen a day. On Mars you must make it from the carbon dioxide air, as NASA's MOXIE machine proved, a little at a time.

Stage 1 · Lift-off from Earth

Everything starts on the ground. A rocket has to climb to about 300 km and reach about 28,000 km an hour to stay in orbit. Counting the push lost to air and gravity, that takes a speed change, called Δv ("delta-v"), of about 9.4 km/s. Most of a rocket is fuel: only a few tonnes in every hundred reach orbit as cargo.

Stage 2 · Fill up in orbit

Your Mars ships reach low Earth orbit with empty tanks: lifting the cargo used up all their fuel. To leave for Mars, each ship must speed up by about 3.6 km/s more. So tankers fly up one by one and pump methane and liquid oxygen across. Here we make refuelling easy: one press docks one tanker.

Boil-off is a real headache. Liquid oxygen boils at −183 °C and liquid methane at −162 °C, so sunlight slowly warms the tanks and some fuel turns to gas that has to be let out. The longer you wait between tankers, the more you lose. Nobody has yet moved large amounts of these super-cold liquids between two ships in orbit; it is one of the big tests still to come.

Stage 3 · Prove it at the Moon

Before betting lives on Mars, test the whole base where help is close. The Moon is about 3 days away: Apollo 11 left on 16 July 1969 and reached lunar orbit on 19 July. Mars is months away, and it can only be reached when the planets line up, about every two years. A broken pump on the Moon is a nuisance; on Mars, with no spare, it can end the mission.

You will land a 20-tonne test base at the Moon's south pole. Getting there from low Earth orbit takes about 4.0 km/s to reach low lunar orbit and about 1.9 km/s more to land. The crater floors near the pole never see sunlight and stay around −220 °C, cold enough to trap ice for billions of years. India's Chandrayaan-1 (its Moon Mineralogy Mapper, built by NASA) saw that ice on the surface, and NASA's LCROSS probe crashed into Cabeus crater in 2009 and threw up a plume that was about 5.6% water by mass.

Stage 4 · Wait for the window, then cruise

You cannot fly to Mars whenever you like. The cheapest path, a Hohmann transfer, is half an oval that touches Earth's orbit on one side of the Sun and Mars's orbit on the other. You must leave when Mars is about 44° ahead of Earth, so that Mars arrives at the far end of the oval at the same moment you do. That lining-up comes round about every 26 months. Stage 5 · Land

Mars's air is only about 1% as thick as Earth's: enough to burn up a careless ship, too thin to slow it down fully. Landers use a heat shield, then parachutes or rocket engines, to drop from about 20,000 km an hour to walking pace in a few minutes. Every landing so far has been run by computers on board, because a radio message takes many minutes each way. Fly the descent itself, step by step, in the Mars Landing Lab.

Stage 6 · Build the colony

You are down. Now keep everyone alive and make the base grow. Each sol (a Mars day, 24 h 39 min) the crew breathes about 0.84 kg of oxygen each, eats about 1.8 kg of food (counting the water in it) and loses a little water, even though the recycler saves about 98% of it, like the one on the space station. Sunlight at Mars is only about 43% as strong as at Earth, and dust storms can block almost all of it. Build machines that make what you need from Mars itself: oxygen from the carbon dioxide air, water from buried ice, food from greenhouses, and fuel for the trip home.

The real things behind the buttons: MOXIE, a toaster-sized machine on the Perseverance rover, made 122 g of oxygen in 16 runs from 2021 to 2023, at best 12 g an hour, proving it can be done. NASA's Kilopower reactor design gives 1 to 10 kW day and night; its ground test, KRUSTY, ran at full power for 28 hours in 2018. Solar panels are cheaper but fear dust: in 2018 a planet-wide storm darkened the sky over the Opportunity rover so much (a dust thickness, or "optical depth", of 10.8) that it never woke up again, and NASA ended its mission in February 2019. Radar from orbit has found buried glaciers of nearly pure ice at middle latitudes. A greenhouse needs roughly 50 m² of crops to feed one person, and 20 to 25 m² gives one person's oxygen; some studies say far more. The Sabatier reaction turns carbon dioxide and hydrogen into methane and water (CO₂ + 4H₂ → CH₄ + 2H₂O); split the water again and you have methane and oxygen, rocket fuel. Bricks of baked regolith piled over the habitat cut the radiation, which on the surface is about 0.67 mSv a sol; NASA lets an astronaut collect at most 600 mSv in a whole career.

      Mission summary

      India and Mars

      DoneMangalyaan, the Mars Orbiter Mission, left Sriharikota on a PSLV on 5 November 2013. It spent about a month raising its orbit round Earth, then cruised for 298 days and slipped into orbit round Mars on 24 September 2014. India became the first country to reach Mars orbit on its first try. It shows the same rule this game uses: you leave in a window, take the slow, cheap path, and arrive months later. Read its story in Mangalyaan.

      PlanA Mars lander. ISRO's next Mars mission, often called Mangalyaan-2 or the Mars Lander Mission, is planned to try India's first landing on Mars, with a launch target of around 2030 reported in November 2025. Talks by ISRO scientists have shown ideas for a supersonic parachute, a sky crane, a small rover and even a helicopter, but these are plans, not final designs.

      PlanBharatiya Antariksh Station. India plans its own space station: the first module, BAS-1 (about 10 tonnes, on an LVM3), by 2028, and five modules by 2035. The first module is meant as a test bed for life support and crew quarters. That is exactly the "test close to home first" idea of Stage 3: a station in low orbit is hours from Earth, so life-support machines can fail there safely and be fixed before anyone trusts them on a trip that lasts years.

      TodayLVM3, India's heaviest rocket, lifts about 640 tonnes off the pad and puts about 8 tonnes into low orbit today, rising to about 10 tonnes with a new upper stage ISRO is building. In Stage 1 you can see why a colony needs far bigger, reusable rockets, or a very long queue of launches.

      For experts: What the page leaves out

      This is a game built on real numbers, not a mission design tool. The big simplifications:

      • Kit masses are guesses. About 11.7 tonnes per person (habitat 4, life support 1.5, power 1.5, rovers and tools 1, food for 400 sols 0.74, building parts 3) plus 8 tonnes of shared equipment. Real design studies vary a lot.
      • One kind of Mars ship. Every rocket choice uses the same 100-tonne-empty ship carrying 100 tonnes, shaped like published Starship numbers. With LVM3 or Falcon Heavy the ships are lifted in pieces, which no one has planned for real. Rockets that are not reusable would also cost far more.
      • One burn, one Δv. The trip is one 3.6 km/s burn from low orbit (4.3 km/s for the fast path). Real windows need a little more or less each time, and arriving needs braking that the landing stage handles only as odds.
      • Boil-off is a flat 0.1% a day of the stored fuel, half-full on average, and launches are spaced 60, 14 or 2 days apart. These are game guesses; real losses depend on sunshades, insulation and coolers.
      • Moon fuel is a choice that adds safety margin, not a modelled mine-and-ship chain. Lunar ice may be much patchier than one crater suggests.
      • Landing odds are made up from your margins, not from an entry simulation.
      • Power is a sol average. No batteries, no night-time gap, no sun angle changes with the seasons except in dust-storm odds. A dust storm cuts sunlight by a simple formula that gives about 3% of normal at the 2018 storm's thickness.
      • Machines are scaled-up guesses. The oxygen plant uses MOXIE's measured rate per unit of electricity, but a big plant would likely do better. The ice drill, greenhouse, brick printer and fuel plant outputs are round numbers in a sensible range. The fuel plant uses 8 kWh of electricity per kg of fuel; the bare chemistry needs about 3, and real plants lose a lot more.
      • Radiation shielding halves the surface dose once every bed is covered with regolith bricks: a simple rule, not a measurement. Solar storms, which can give a big dose in hours, are not modelled.
      • People are simple: one health number for body and mood, 6 working hours a sol each, no illness, no skills.
      • Not included: spacesuits, rovers' trips, perchlorate salts in Mars soil, low gravity's effect on health, communications blackouts at solar conjunction, cost, and the law about who may build on Mars.

      Quiz: five questions

      Answer each one by using the lab above, then check. Good for a class: pairs work through them, the teacher reveals the answers.

      1. How much oxygen does one person breathe in a day, and how much oxygen did NASA's MOXIE make on Mars in total?

        Use the lab: Read the opening of Stage 6 · Build the colony, or find the MOXIE and Oxygen per person rows in How far to trust it.

        Show answer

        About 0.84 kg a day. MOXIE made 122 g in total over 16 runs (2021 to 2023), at best 12 g an hour. That is less than a sixth of what one person breathes in a single day, so a colony needs much bigger oxygen plants.

      2. With a Starship-class rocket and a crew of 4, how much fuel does your Mars ship need for the trip, and how many tanker flights bring it?

        Use the lab: In Stage 1 pick Starship-class and Crew of 4, press Next stage, then read the boxes at the top of Stage 2.

        Show answer

        About 252 t of methane and oxygen for the 3.6 km/s burn (155 t of ship and kit × 1.63), brought by 3 tanker flights of 100 t each.

      3. Why test the base on the Moon before Mars, and how much water did LCROSS find in the soil of Cabeus crater?

        Use the lab: Read the opening of Stage 3 · Prove it at the Moon and its maths box.

        Show answer

        The Moon is only about 3 days away, so spare parts and help can arrive quickly; Mars is months away and reachable only when the planets line up. LCROSS found about 5.6% water by mass, so each tonne of soil holds about 56 kg of water, enough to make about 50 kg of oxygen.

      4. HistoryWhen did India's Mangalyaan leave Earth, how long did it cruise, and when did it reach Mars?

        Use the lab: Read India and Mars.

        Show answer

        It launched on 5 November 2013, cruised for 298 days after leaving Earth's orbit, and entered orbit round Mars on 24 September 2014. India was the first country to reach Mars orbit on its first try.

      5. HistoryWhat happened to the Opportunity rover in 2018, and why does it matter for a Mars colony's power?

        Use the lab: Read the second paragraph of Stage 6 · Build the colony, and find the Dust storm thickness row in How far to trust it.

        Show answer

        A planet-wide dust storm darkened the sky (optical depth 10.8) so much that Opportunity's solar panels could not keep it alive; it never woke up and NASA ended the mission in February 2019. A colony that relies only on solar power can lose almost all of it for weeks, which is why a fission reactor, which works day and night, is useful.
