Solar System Lab

The planets are where JPL's own orbital elements put them on the date you choose, back to Aryabhata's sky in 499 CE. Pick a world to see NASA's figures for it, the cost of sending a Starship there, and then try to land on it yourself. Every attempt is logged, so you can learn each world by trial and error.

In simple words

A map of every planet, placed where NASA’s numbers say it is on any date you choose, even back to Aryabhata’s sky in the year 499. Pick a world to see its facts, find out what it would take to send a Starship there, and then try landing on it yourself.

Try this first: Under Pick a world, choose a world such as Mars or the Moon. Then go to Landing trials, press Start descent, and hold Hold to fire to slow down before you hit the ground.

  • Mars’s pull is weaker than Earth’s: a person there would weigh a bit more than a third of what they weigh at home.
  • Landing on Mars takes less rocket effort than landing on the Moon, even though Mars is much farther away, because Mars’s air does most of the braking.
  • Earth and Mars both circle the Sun at different speeds, so the distance between them keeps changing: very far apart in 499, much closer on the day of Apollo 11.

Quiz: five questions

Answer each one by using the lab above, then check. Good for a class: pairs work through them, the teacher reveals the answers.

  1. How strong is gravity on the surface of Mars?

    Use the lab: Mars is picked when the page opens. Look at the NASA fact sheet table and read the row Surface gravity.

    Show answer

    3.7 m/s², which is 0.38 × Earth. A person would weigh a bit more than a third of their Earth weight.

  2. Which needs a bigger total change in speed from Earth orbit: landing on the Moon or landing on Mars?

    Use the lab: Under Pick a world, click Moon and read Total from Earth orbit in the Sending a Starship table. Then click Mars and read the same row.

    Show answer

    The Moon needs 6.25 km/s; Mars needs only 4.34 km/s. Mars is much farther away, but its air does most of the braking (the landing step is just 0.75 km/s). On the airless Moon, the ship must burn 3.10 km/s to stop and land.

  3. What is the heaviest payload one refilled Starship could land on Titan?

    Use the lab: Click Titan. Read the last row, Most payload a refilled Starship could carry. Then move the Payload to the surface slider from 50 t to 100 t and watch the verdict.

    Show answer

    About 81 t. At 50 t the verdict says Possible (about 1,318 t of propellant). At 100 t it says Not in one ship, because the propellant needed is more than the 1,550 t tank holds.

  4. Mercury is much closer to us than Mars. Why does the lab say a Starship could not land on Mercury in one go?

    Use the lab: Click Mercury. Compare Arrival speed relative to the target and the row Braking burn all the way down, no air with the same rows for Mars.

    Show answer

    Mercury needs 16.61 km/s in total, so the verdict is Not in one ship. Falling in towards the Sun, the ship arrives very fast (9.61 km/s relative to Mercury), and Mercury has no air. So the engines must cancel all of it: 11.06 km/s of braking.

  5. HistoryThe lab can show the sky of Aryabhata's time. How far apart were Earth and Mars on that date, and how does it compare with the day of Apollo 11?

    Use the lab: In the orrery, click the Aryabhata, 499 CE button and read the line Earth to Mars on this date. Then click Apollo 11 and read it again.

    Show answer

    About 335 million km in 499 CE, but only about 88 million km on the Apollo 11 date (1969). Both planets go round the Sun at different speeds, so the gap between them keeps changing.
