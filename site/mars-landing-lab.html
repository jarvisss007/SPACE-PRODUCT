Curiosity · Perseverance · seven minutes of terror

Mars Landing Lab

A rover reaching Mars is going about 5.8 km/s, and seven minutes later it has to be sitting still on the ground. Radio signals take longer than that to reach Earth, so nobody can help: the spacecraft does it alone. Mars has air, but only about 1% as much as Earth. That is enough to burn up a spacecraft, but not enough to stop it. So it takes three tricks in a row: a heat shield, the biggest supersonic parachute ever flown on another planet, and a rocket-powered “sky crane” that lowers the rover on cables.

Fly the sky crane

The parachute has done what it can. At about 1.9 km up and nearly 90 m/s, the rover and its rocket stage drop out of the backshell. Eight engines, each able to throttle between 400 and 3,100 newtons, must stop the fall before 400 kg of propellant runs out. Then, about 20 m up, the stage lowers the rover on 6.4 m of cable and sets it down gently.

Press the button and keep the falling speed in check. Below 20 m, come down slower than 1 m/s; that is this page’s pass mark for setting the rover down on its wheels.

Checks against the real landings

What the model leaves out

Mars’s air is modelled as a simple exponential with the NASA fact-sheet surface density and scale height, and the ground is put at the reference level; Jezero and Gale craters both lie a few kilometres below it, which gives real landers a little more air to work with. The heat shield’s drag and lift are held constant and the steering is a single fixed roll, where the real guidance rolled back and forth to hit a 20 km target. The parachute’s drag coefficient and the descent engines’ efficiency (specific impulse 220 s) are assumed values, not published figures.

Quiz: five questions

Answer each one by using the lab above, then check. Good for a class: pairs work through them, the teacher reveals the answers.

  1. With the starting settings, when does the parachute open, how high up, and at what Mach number?

    Use the lab: Load the page and leave the three sliders where they are (entry angle 15.5°, steering 45°, parachute at Mach 2.04). Read the stat tiles under The descent, second by second.

    Show answer

    About 3.9 min after entry, 15.1 km up, at Mach 2.04. The verdict says “Ready for the sky crane”, because the parachute opened below its Mach 2.2 limit and high enough to slow down.

  2. What happens if the rover comes in too shallow, or too steep?

    Use the lab: Drag Entry angle at 125 km, below level all the way down to 10.0° and read the verdict. Then drag it up to 22.0° and read the Peak braking and Parachute opens tiles.

    Show answer

    At 10.0° it skips out: it bounces off the thin upper air and leaves Mars. At 22.0° it still reaches the sky crane, but peak braking jumps to about 17.7 g (from 10.0 g), and the parachute opens at about 2.0 min. Steeper means it hits thick air faster, so it brakes harder.

  3. What goes wrong if you open the parachute too early, while the rover is still very fast?

    Use the lab: Move Open the parachute at up to Mach 2.50 and read the verdict box.

    Show answer

    Parachute shredded. It opens at Mach 2.50, but it is rated for Mach 2.2 at most. Faster air would rip it apart.

  4. Why does the steering setting decide whether the rover survives?

    Use the lab: Set Steering: how far the lift is rolled away from straight up to 0° and read the tiles. Then set it to 180° and read the verdict.

    Show answer

    At 0° (lift up) the parachute opens about 5.8 min in, at about 13.1 km. At 180° (lift down) the verdict is “Crashed under the heat shield”. Lift pointed down makes it dive, so it reaches the ground before the thin air has slowed it enough to open the parachute.

  5. HistoryThe page checks its model against the real Curiosity and Perseverance landings. How close does it get for peak heating and for leaving the backshell?

    Use the lab: Scroll to the table Checks against the real landings and read the rows Peak heating after entry and Leaving the backshell.

    Show answer

    Curiosity’s real peak heating came at 1 min 15 s; the model gives 66 s. Perseverance left the backshell at under 320 km/h at 1.9 km; the model gives 312 km/h at 1.9 km. So the simple model lands close to the real missions.
