Curiosity · Perseverance · seven minutes of terror

Mars Landing Lab

A rover reaching Mars is going about 5.8 km/s, and seven minutes later it has to be sitting still on the ground. Radio signals take longer than that to reach Earth, so nobody can help: the spacecraft does it alone. Mars has air, but only about 1% as much as Earth. That is enough to burn up a spacecraft, but not enough to stop it. So it takes three tricks in a row: a heat shield, the biggest supersonic parachute ever flown on another planet, and a rocket-powered “sky crane” that lowers the rover on cables.

Fly the sky crane

The parachute has done what it can. At about 1.9 km up and nearly 90 m/s, the rover and its rocket stage drop out of the backshell. Eight engines, each able to throttle between 400 and 3,100 newtons, must stop the fall before 400 kg of propellant runs out. Then, about 20 m up, the stage lowers the rover on 6.4 m of cable and sets it down gently.

Press the button and keep the falling speed in check. Below 20 m, come down slower than 1 m/s; that is this page’s pass mark for setting the rover down on its wheels.

What the model leaves out

Mars’s air is modelled as a simple exponential with the NASA fact-sheet surface density and scale height, and the ground is put at the reference level; Jezero and Gale craters both lie a few kilometres below it, which gives real landers a little more air to work with. The heat shield’s drag and lift are held constant and the steering is a single fixed roll, where the real guidance rolled back and forth to hit a 20 km target. The parachute’s drag coefficient and the descent engines’ efficiency (specific impulse 220 s) are assumed values, not published figures.
