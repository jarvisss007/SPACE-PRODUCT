In simple words

See how a black hole bends light around itself, meet the real black holes we know, and find out what would happen to a spaceship that falls in. It ends with wormholes, which are allowed on paper but have never been seen.

Try this first: Drag the Viewing angle from the disk’s axis slider to see the glowing disk bend around the black hole. Then click a row in The black holes we know to send the Starship toward it.

  • The dark middle, called the shadow, is where light falls in and never gets out. It looks about two and a half times wider than the black hole's point of no return, the event horizon.
  • Near a small black hole, gravity pulls your feet so much harder than your head that you are stretched apart before you reach the edge. At a giant one, like the huge black hole in the middle of the galaxy M87, you would cross the edge without feeling anything.
  • A wormhole you could travel through would need strange negative energy to hold it open. No wormhole has ever been seen.

Wormholes: allowed on paper, never seen

In 1935 Einstein and Rosen found that the black-hole solution links two sheets of space by a bridge. It pinches shut faster than light can cross it. In 1988 Michael Morris and Kip Thorne asked what a wormhole you could actually travel through would need. The answer: the throat must be held open by matter with negative energy density, which violates the energy conditions every ordinary material obeys.

The view above is a wormhole of the simplest traversable kind (the Ellis–Morris–Thorne throat). Inside the ring you see the sky of the other end; outside it, our sky is lensed around the throat.

Exotic matter estimate: of order c²·a/G for a throat of radius a (Visser, Lorentzian Wormholes, 1995), about 0.7 Jupiter masses of negative energy per metre of throat. The only negative energy ever measured, the Casimir effect between metal plates, is many orders of magnitude too small. No wormhole has ever been observed.

Quiz: five questions

Answer each one by using the lab above, then check. Good for a class: pairs work through them, the teacher reveals the answers.

  1. How big is a black hole's dark shadow compared with its event horizon?

    Use the lab: Look at the readouts beside the ray-traced picture at the top. Read Shadow edge (photon capture) and Photon sphere.

    Show answer

    The shadow edge is 2.60 rs, about 2.6 times the horizon radius. Light can orbit at the photon sphere, 1.5 rs, and the disk starts at 3 rs.

  2. A 1 g ship flies to Gaia BH1 and to M87*. How long does each trip take for the crew, and for people at home?

    Use the lab: In The black holes we know, click the Gaia BH1 row and read Getting there at 1 g under the charts. Then click the M87* row.

    Show answer

    Gaia BH1: 14.3 years aboard, about 1,562 years at home. M87*: 34.6 years aboard, but 55.0 million years at home. The table's 1 g ship, crew years column shows the same crew times.

  3. Once inside the horizon, how long could you last at Sagittarius A* compared with M87*?

    Use the lab: Click the Sagittarius A* row and read Longest you can survive inside the horizon. Then click the M87* row.

    Show answer

    Sagittarius A*: about 66.5 s. M87*: about 27.9 hours. M87* is about 1,500 times heavier, so its horizon is bigger and the fall to the centre takes longer.

  4. Why would you be torn apart long before reaching Gaia BH1, but cross M87*'s horizon without feeling anything?

    Use the lab: Click Gaia BH1 and read A person is torn apart (10 g stretch) at and the verdict. Do the same for M87*.

    Show answer

    At Gaia BH1 the stretch reaches 10 g about 3,605 km from the centre, about 127 horizon radii out, because its horizon is only 28 km. M87*'s horizon is 128 AU, so you reach it while still far from the centre, where the tides are gentle. The verdict says You would cross the horizon intact.

  5. HistoryWho first showed that general relativity allows a bridge (wormhole), and who later asked what a travellable one would need?

    Use the lab: Read the text in Wormholes: allowed on paper, never seen. Then look at Negative energy needed for the starting Throat radius of 1.0 m.

    Show answer

    Einstein and Rosen found the bridge in 1935, but it pinches shut faster than light can cross it. In 1988 Michael Morris and Kip Thorne showed that a travellable throat needs matter with negative energy density. Even a 1 m throat needs about 0.71 Jupiter masses of it. No wormhole has ever been observed.
