Event Horizon Telescope · 2017 to now

Photographing a Black Hole

On 10 April 2019 the world saw the first picture of a black hole: a glowing orange ring around a dark middle, in the galaxy M87. On 12 May 2022 came a second one, Sagittarius A*, the black hole at the centre of our own Milky Way. No single telescope took them. Eight radio dishes on four continents watched at the same moment and worked as one dish almost as wide as the Earth. This page lets you work out why it had to be that big, check the ring size yourself with one formula, see where the dishes are, and learn why the data travelled by aeroplane. For what black holes are and what happens if you fall in, see the Black Hole and Wormhole Lab.

In simple words

No telescope on Earth is big enough to see a black hole on its own. So astronomers linked radio dishes across the planet into one giant telescope almost as wide as the Earth, and in 2019 and 2022 they took the first pictures of two black holes.

Try this first: Under Make it sharper, press Your eye, then EHT (10,700 km) and Radio (1.3 mm), and watch the smudge turn into a ring. Then press Sgr A* under How big is the shadow?

  • The black hole rings look tiny from Earth: about as big as an orange or a doughnut on the Moon would look to us.
  • Einstein's gravity predicts the size of the dark middle from the black hole's mass and distance, and the pictures match.
  • The 2017 recordings filled about a thousand hard drives, which were flown to two supercomputers because the internet was far too slow.

Make it sharper

Every telescope blurs. The smallest detail it can see is about θ ≈ 1.22 × λ ÷ D: the wavelength of the light (λ) divided by the width of the telescope (D). Smaller is sharper. The black hole rings are tiny: the M87* ring is about 42 microarcseconds across (a microarcsecond is a millionth of a 3,600th of a degree). Pick a telescope and a kind of light, then see what the ring would look like.

The picture is a model ring blurred by the telescope's sharpness, not real data. The blur spot drawn in the corner is the size of θ. For your eye the page uses a 5 mm pupil; real eyes do worse, about 1 arcminute.

How big is the shadow?

Einstein's gravity predicts how big the dark middle should look. Light that passes too close is swallowed; light that just misses is bent around. Seen from far away, the edge of the dark patch (the "shadow") has a diameter of 2√27 × GM/c², about 10.4 times a length called the gravitational radius, GM/c². Divide by the distance and you get the angle. For Sgr A*, nobody tuned this to fit the picture: the mass came from stars seen orbiting it. For M87*, the 6.5 billion Suns figure was itself worked out by the EHT from the ring, so it is bound to fit. The older estimate from stars moving in M87 (about 6.2 billion Suns) gives about 38 µas, still close to the 42 µas ring.

    A telescope as big as the Earth

    You cannot build a dish 10,000 km wide. But two dishes far apart, recording the same radio waves with atomic clocks, can act like two small pieces of one giant dish. Each pair is called a baseline. The more pairs, pointing in more directions, the more of the giant dish is "filled in". Choose a year, then tap a site to see its baselines and how long they are.

    What about India?

    India has no EHT station. The EHT works with waves about 1.3 mm long, and water vapour in the air soaks those up, so its dishes sit on very high, very dry sites: Chile's Atacama desert, Hawaii's Mauna Kea, the South Pole. India's big radio telescopes listen to much longer waves instead:

    GMRT, the Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope near Khodad, about 80 km north of Pune: 30 dishes, each 45 m wide, spread over about 25 km. It hears waves from about 20 cm to 2.5 m long (about 120 to 1,450 MHz) and is one of the world's largest telescopes for metre waves. It studies pulsars, hydrogen gas in far galaxies and the early universe.

    Ooty Radio Telescope, near Udhagamandalam: one long trough-shaped dish, 530 m long and 30 m wide, at 326.5 MHz (waves about 92 cm long). It lies on a hillside that slopes 11°, the same as Ooty's latitude, so its long side points parallel to the Earth's axis and it can follow the sky just by turning. Both are run by NCRA-TIFR in Pune. A 2020 proposal from ISRO's Space Applications Centre and the Harvard–Smithsonian CfA suggested a small millimetre dish at Hanle in Ladakh (4,500 m high) to fill the EHT's gap over Asia; as far as this page found, it has not been built.

    Data by aeroplane

    In April 2017 each site recorded the raw radio waves at a furious rate. Together the sites filled about 5 petabytes (5 million gigabytes) on roughly 1,000 sealed hard drives filled with helium, because ordinary drives fail in the thin air of mountain tops. That is more than half a tonne of drives. They were flown to two supercomputers called correlators: MIT Haystack Observatory near Boston and the Max Planck Institute for Radio Astronomy in Bonn, Germany. The South Pole drives had to wait for the polar winter to end; they reached Haystack on 13 December 2017. Could the internet have done it faster? Pick a connection.

      For experts: How far to trust it

      Each number the page computes, set against what the EHT and other sources published:

      The computed shadow is about 5% smaller than the M87* ring and about 2% bigger than the Sgr A* ring. Both differences are inside the published error bars. The bright ring is expected to be a little larger than the shadow edge, because the glowing gas sits just outside it; the EHT calibrated that factor with computer simulations. The ring is only about 1.4 times wider than the 1.22λ/D blur, so the pictures rely on careful image-making methods (which is how the EHT gets "under 20 µas"), and the EHT checked them by having four separate teams make images without talking to each other. The straight line from Spain to the South Pole is longer than 10,700 km, but the South Pole cannot see M87 and Spain sees Sgr A* only low in the sky, so the longest useful baselines are shorter. Popular "on the Moon" comparisons vary (orange, bagel, baseball, doughnut); the page computes its own.

      For experts: What the page leaves out

      The blur model treats the array as one perfect round dish. A real array has gaps between dishes, so its blur is an odd, patchy shape, and the picture has to be filled in by imaging programs that make smart guesses. The page also ignores the Earth's turning (which sweeps each baseline across the sky and fills in more of the giant dish), the atmosphere's wobbles, and the fact that the South Pole cannot see M87 at all. Baseline lengths on the map are straight lines through the Earth between the sites; what matters for sharpness is the length as seen from the black hole, which changes through the night. The map is hand-drawn and rough. Sgr A* changes in minutes, much faster than M87*, which made its picture harder; the page does not show that. The VLA never observes at 1.3 mm (its shortest waves are about 7 mm), and visible-light interferometry across the whole Earth is not possible today; those settings are there to compare, not because anyone uses them.

      Quiz: five questions

      Answer each one by using the lab above, then check. Good for a class: pairs work through them, the teacher reveals the answers.

      1. Why did the Event Horizon Telescope need to be almost as wide as the Earth?

        Use the lab: Under Make it sharper, press Radio (1.3 mm), then try 10 m telescope, VLA (36 km) and EHT (10,700 km). Read Blur ÷ ring.

        Show answer

        Sharpness is about 1.22 × wavelength ÷ telescope width. At 1.3 mm even the 36 km VLA blurs about 9,000 µas, hundreds of times bigger than the 42 µas ring. Only a 10,700 km telescope gets the blur down to about 31 µas, smaller than the ring, so the dark middle shows.

      2. Using Einstein's formula, how big should the shadow of Sgr A* look, and does it match the picture?

        Use the lab: In How big is the shadow?, press Sgr A* and read Computed shadow and Measured ring.

        Show answer

        With 4.3 million Suns at 8.3 kpc, the formula gives about 53 µas. The EHT measured 51.8 ± 2.3 µas, so it matches within the error.

      3. M87* is about 1,500 times heavier than Sgr A*. Why do the two rings look almost the same size?

        Use the lab: Switch between M87* and Sgr A* in How big is the shadow? and read the verdict below the chart.

        Show answer

        M87 is also about 2,000 times farther away. The shadow's size grows with mass but its angle shrinks with distance, so the two effects almost cancel: about 40 µas against about 53 µas.

      4. How long would it take to send the 5 petabytes from 2017 over a 100 Mbit/s home connection?

        Use the lab: In Data by aeroplane, press Home broadband (100 Mbit/s) and read Time to move it all.

        Show answer

        About 12.7 years, running non-stop. Flying about 1,000 hard drives took days, which is why the data travelled by aeroplane to MIT Haystack and Bonn.

      5. HistoryWhen were the first pictures of M87* and Sgr A* shown to the world, and which one came first?

        Use the lab: Read the What came next timeline.

        Show answer

        M87* came first, on 10 April 2019. Sgr A*, the black hole at the centre of our own galaxy, followed on 12 May 2022. Both used data recorded in April 2017.
