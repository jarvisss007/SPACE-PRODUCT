India · private space · 2020 to now

India's Space Startups

Until 2020, almost everything India sent to space was built and launched by ISRO. That year the government opened space to private companies. Six years later, more than 300 Indian space startups are registered, one of them has put its own rocket into orbit, and others fly cameras that see crop health, radar that sees through monsoon clouds, and satellites that watch space junk. This page tracks the main ones, what they have actually done, what is still a plan, and what it could mean for India, with the numbers and where they come from.

In simple words

Since 2020, private Indian companies have been allowed to build rockets and satellites. This page shows who they are, what they have really done, and how much India's space business could grow.

Try this first: Under Who does what, tap Skyroot Aerospace, then Pixxel. Then press Goal pace on the growth chart.

  • In July 2026, Skyroot's Vikram-1 became the first rocket built by a private Indian company to reach orbit.
  • Startup satellites already in space can spot sick crops, see through monsoon clouds and track space junk.
  • India's goal is to grow its space business about five times, from $8.4 billion in 2022 to $44 billion by 2033. That needs about 16% growth every single year.

The road to $44 billion

India's space economy was about $8.4 billion in 2022, roughly 2% of the world's. IN-SPACe, the government body that authorises private space activity, has set a goal of about $44 billion by 2033, about 8% of the world's. How fast does it have to grow? Move the slider.

    What it means for people

    Farmers

    Hyperspectral cameras (like Pixxel's) see hundreds of colours, not three, so they can spot stressed or diseased crops and dry soil before the eye can.

    Floods and cyclones

    Radar (like GalaxEye's) sees through clouds and at night, exactly when monsoon floods and cyclones hide the ground from ordinary cameras.

    Safer space

    Satellites that track junk (like Digantara's) warn operators before a crash, protecting the satellites that give us weather, TV and navigation.

    Cheaper, more launches

    With private rockets, India is no longer limited to the number of rockets ISRO can build in a year, and small satellites need not wait for a ride.

    Jobs and skills

    Engineers who once had to go abroad or join ISRO can now build rockets in Hyderabad and Chennai. Skyroot alone reports more than 1,000 staff.

    ISRO freed up

    When industry builds the everyday rockets (HAL and L&T now make PSLVs and HAL owns SSLV), ISRO can focus on Gaganyaan, the Moon and new science.

    For experts: What the page leaves out

    Only a dozen of the 300+ companies appear here, chosen because they have flown hardware or are building rockets. Many others make ground stations, software, parts and data products (for example SatSure, Ananth Technologies, Kawa Space and others); leaving them out does not mean they matter less. The page does not judge whether any company is a good investment, and does not give live updates: check the date in the sources. Defence space work, and large established firms such as L&T, Godrej and HAL, are mentioned only where they join the startup story.

    Quiz: five questions

    Answer each one by using the lab above, then check. Good for a class: pairs work through them, the teacher reveals the answers.

    1. Which Indian private company was first to put a rocket into orbit, and when?

      Use the lab: Tap Skyroot Aerospace under Who does what, or read the timeline in What changed since 2020.

      Show answer

      Skyroot Aerospace: its Vikram-1 reached a 450 km orbit on 18 July 2026, on its first try. That made India the third country, after the USA and China, where a private company has launched to orbit.

    2. How fast must India's space economy grow every year to go from $8.4 billion in 2022 to $44 billion in 2033?

      Use the lab: Press Goal pace on The road to $44 billion chart and read Pace the goal needs.

      Show answer

      About 16.2% a year, every year for eleven years. At that pace the industry doubles roughly every 4.6 years.

    3. Which startup satellite can see through monsoon clouds and at night, and why?

      Use the lab: Tap GalaxEye and read What it means for people.

      Show answer

      GalaxEye's Drishti (launched 3 May 2026) carries a radar. Radar sends its own radio waves and times the echoes, so it does not need sunlight and passes through clouds. It also carries an ordinary camera.

    4. Startups raised about $200 million in 2025. Roughly what share is that of the Department of Space's yearly budget?

      Use the lab: Look at the bars in The money going in.

      Show answer

      About ₹1,740 crore against ₹13,706 crore for the Department of Space in 2026–27: roughly 13%. Startup money is growing fast (it was $43 million in 2021) but the government is still by far the biggest spender.

    5. HistoryWhat did the government change in June 2020 that started this whole story?

      Use the lab: Read the first entry in What changed since 2020.

      Show answer

      It set up IN-SPACe, which gives private companies permission to build and launch. Before that, almost everything India sent to space was built and launched by ISRO.
