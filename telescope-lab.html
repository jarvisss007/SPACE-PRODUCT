Hubble · Webb · India’s AstroSat · the wave nature of light

Telescope Lab

No telescope can show a point of light as a perfect point. Light is a wave, and passing through a round mirror spreads each star into a tiny disc with faint rings, the “Airy pattern”. The bigger the mirror, the smaller the disc; the longer the wavelength, the bigger it gets. Two stars closer together than about one disc width merge into one blob. That single rule decides what every telescope can and cannot see.

In simple words

Why bigger telescopes see sharper: the bigger the mirror, the finer the detail it can make out.

Try this first: Move the sliders for colour and separation, and watch two stars blur together or split apart.

  • Light spreads out a little as it enters a telescope. A bigger mirror spreads it less, so the picture is sharper.
  • Hubble sees far finer detail than your eye, but even it cannot see the flags left on the Moon.
  • Webb sees heat light (infrared), so it must be kept very cold, or its own warmth would blind it.

Why Webb has to be so cold

Everything warm glows. A telescope at room temperature glows most strongly at about 10 µm, right in the middle of the infrared that Webb was built to see, so a warm Webb would be dazzled by itself. Its sunshield keeps the mirror below 50 K, which moves its own glow far out of the way. Drag to see where an object’s glow peaks.

Then and now

Four hundred years of bigger mirrors, from a spectacle maker’s shop to a 39-metre giant. Tap a year to open it; green dots mark India’s part.

  1. 1608–09The first telescopes

    In 1608 Hans Lipperhey, a Dutch spectacle maker, asked for a patent on a tube with two lenses that made far things look near. In 1609 Galileo built his own, pointed it at the sky and saw mountains on the Moon and four moons circling Jupiter.

  2. 1668Newton swaps the lens for a mirror

    Lenses bend each colour a little differently, so bright stars got rainbow edges. Isaac Newton used a curved mirror instead, and nearly every big research telescope since has been a mirror telescope.

  3. 1781Herschel finds a new planet

    William Herschel ground his own mirrors and, while mapping stars, found Uranus. It was the first planet ever discovered with a telescope.

  4. 1917–29Mount Wilson’s 100-inch and Edwin Hubble

    The 2.5 m Hooker telescope in California was the biggest in the world until 1948. With it Edwin Hubble showed that the Andromeda “nebula” is a whole separate galaxy (1923–24), and then that galaxies are rushing apart: the universe is expanding.

  5. 1948Palomar’s 200-inch

    The 5 m Hale telescope, twice as wide as Mount Wilson’s, was dedicated in June 1948. It led the hunt for faraway galaxies and quasars for decades.

  6. 1986The Vainu Bappu TelescopeIndia

    A 2.3 m telescope at Kavalur in Tamil Nadu, built in India, was unveiled on 6 January 1986. It is named after astronomer Vainu Bappu, who started the project but died in 1982 before it was finished.

  7. 1990–93Hubble goes up, then gets glasses

    Hubble’s 2.4 m mirror was launched above the blurring air in April 1990, but it had been polished to slightly the wrong shape. In December 1993 astronauts fitted correcting optics, and its pictures became razor sharp.

  8. 1993Keck: a mirror made of tiles

    One 10 m mirror would sag under its own weight, so Keck in Hawaii uses 36 six-sided pieces that act as one. Almost every giant telescope since copies the idea.

  9. 2015AstroSat, India’s space observatoryIndia

    Launched by ISRO’s PSLV on 28 September 2015, AstroSat watches the sky in ultraviolet light and X-rays at once. Its ultraviolet telescope is one of the telescopes in this lab.

  10. 2016–22Devasthal: a 3.6 m mirror and a liquid oneIndia

    The 3.6 m Devasthal Optical Telescope near Nainital, built with Belgium, was switched on on 30 March 2016, India’s biggest steerable telescope. Next to it, the 4 m International Liquid Mirror Telescope, a spinning pool of mercury that stares straight up, saw first light in about April 2022.

  11. 2021Webb launches

    The James Webb Space Telescope left Earth on 25 December 2021. Its 6.5 m gold mirror unfolded in space and is kept colder than 50 K so it can see faint infrared light from the first galaxies.

  12. 2025Rubin’s first look

    On 23 June 2025 the Vera C. Rubin Observatory in Chile showed its first images, taken with the largest digital camera ever built (3.2 gigapixels). It will photograph the whole southern sky every few nights for ten years.

  13. NowThe next giants

    Europe’s Extremely Large Telescope in Chile, with a 39 m mirror made of 798 pieces, is being built and plans first light in 2029. The Thirty Meter Telescope, an India partner project whose mirror pieces are partly made in India, is still stuck: building in Hawaii has stalled for years, and in 2025 the US science agency NSF left it out of its budget plans.

For experts: Checks
For experts: What the model leaves out

The images assume a perfect, unobstructed round mirror. Real telescopes have a secondary mirror and its supports in the way, which add spikes, and Webb’s hexagonal segments give its famous six-pointed stars. Distances to Mars and Pluto are typical close-approach values. AstroSat’s ultraviolet telescope is shown at its 38 cm aperture; detector pixels can limit real images before diffraction does. Peak glow wavelengths come from Wien’s law for an ideal black body.

Quiz: five questions

Answer each one by using the lab above, then check. Good for a class: pairs work through them, the teacher reveals the answers.

  1. How much more light does Webb gather than Hubble?

    Use the lab: Click the Webb button and read Light gathered, compared with Hubble's 4.0 m² under What it can make out.

    Show answer

    25.4 m², about 6.4 times Hubble's 4.0 m². A bigger mirror collects more light, so it can see fainter objects.

  2. Could Hubble see the Apollo lunar module left on the Moon?

    Use the lab: Click Hubble and leave the Colour of light observed slider at its starting value (about 501 nm). Read the Smallest detail for The Moon in the table.

    Show answer

    No. Hubble's smallest detail on the Moon is about 98 m, but the lunar module is only 4 m wide. It is far smaller than one blur disc.

  3. For Hubble, how does the sharpest detail change when you switch from violet light to red light?

    Use the lab: Click Hubble. Move the Colour of light observed slider to about 400 nm (violet) and read Sharpest detail at this colour. Then move it to about 700 nm (red) and read it again.

    Show answer

    About 42 milliarcseconds in violet and about 73 milliarcseconds in red. The blur disc is 1.22 λ/D, so a longer wavelength gives a bigger disc and a less sharp image.

  4. Why must Webb be kept colder than 50 K?

    Use the lab: In Why Webb has to be so cold, set Temperature of the glowing object to 300 K and read where the glow peaks. Then set it to 50 K.

    Show answer

    At 300 K the glow peaks at about 9.7 µm, inside Webb's 0.6–28.5 µm range, so a warm Webb would be dazzled by itself. At 50 K the peak moves to about 58 µm, out of the way. Peak wavelength = 2,898 ÷ temperature (Wien's law).

  5. HistoryHubble (1990), India's AstroSat (2015) and Webb (2021) were launched years apart. Which has the biggest mirror, and can AstroSat UVIT split the two stars that Hubble splits?

    Use the lab: Click each of Hubble, AstroSat UVIT and Webb and read the Mirror line. Keep both sliders at their starting values and read the message under the star picture for Hubble and for AstroSat UVIT.

    Show answer

    Mirrors: Hubble 2.4 m, AstroSat UVIT 38 cm, Webb 6.5 m, the biggest. Hubble shows Two stars (1.2 disc-widths apart). AstroSat UVIT shows One blob (0.19 disc-widths), because its smaller mirror makes a bigger blur disc.
