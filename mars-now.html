Mars Now

Two robots are driving on Mars today: Perseverance in Jezero crater and Curiosity in Gale crater. What time is it where they are? What season? How many Mars days have they worked? And how long does a radio message take to reach them? This page works all of that out from the clock in your device, using the same maths NASA's Mars24 clock uses. What time is it on Mars?

A day on Mars is called a sol. It is a little longer than ours: 24 hours, 39 minutes and 35 seconds. Mars has a "world clock" like Earth's: Mars Coordinated Time is the average sun time on Mars's zero line of longitude, the way Greenwich time is on Earth. Each rover keeps the time of its own place, and the true sun time can run ahead or behind the average by up to about 50 minutes, because Mars's orbit is quite oval.

Which sol, which season?

Each rover counts its sols from the day it landed, which is sol 0. Curiosity landed on 6 August 2012 (UTC) and Perseverance on 18 February 2021. Mars years are counted too: Mars Year 1 began on 11 April 1955, at the start of spring in Mars's northern half. One Mars year lasts about 687 Earth days, or 669 sols.

How far away is Mars?

Earth goes round the Sun in one year, Mars in almost two, so the gap between them keeps changing. When Earth overtakes Mars on the inside track, Mars is closest and opposite the Sun in our sky: an opposition. When they are on opposite sides of the Sun, Mars is farthest and the Sun sits in the way of the radio link: a solar conjunction. Then NASA stops sending commands for about two weeks, because the Sun's hot gas can scramble the signals. Drag the slider to move through time.

Light, and radio, travel at 299,792 km every second. Nothing can go faster, so Mars can never be "live". When Perseverance touched down at about 20:44 UTC on 18 Feb 2021, the news took about 11 minutes to reach Earth; mission control heard it at 20:55.

For experts: How far to trust it

The page does its own maths, so we test it against answers other people worked out and published. The numbers in the "This page" column are calculated live, right now, by the same code that runs the clocks above.

The clocks follow NASA GISS's Mars24 method (Allison and McEwen, 2000, with Mars24's later small fixes). It should match Mars24 to within a second or so. The planet positions use JPL's simple "approximate positions" formulas, which JPL says are good to about 25,000 km for Mars and 6,000 km for Earth between the years 1800 and 2050. That is tiny next to distances of 55 to 400 million km, which is why the slider stops before 2050.

For experts: What the page leaves out

It does not know where the rovers have driven since landing. Both have moved some kilometres (Curiosity has climbed Mount Sharp), but a few kilometres changes their clocks by only seconds. Sol counts use each mission's own clock, which was fixed before landing for a spot a tiny bit away from where each rover actually touched down. The page does not use live data, so it cannot tell you if a rover is awake, busy or in trouble. Sunrise and sunset are rough (about 6 in the morning and 6 in the evening), not worked out for the hills around each crater. Distances are from the centre of Earth to the centre of Mars. The solar conjunction shown is the moment the Sun is most directly in the way; the real pause in commands lasts about two weeks around it and NASA sets the exact dates each time. After 2027 your device would also need to know about any new leap seconds, and the page assumes none.
