Kundli Lab

A kundli is a map of the sky at the moment you were born: where the Sun, the Moon, the five planets and the two lunar nodes, Rahu and Ketu, stood among the 12 rashis and 27 nakshatras, and which rashi was rising in the east (the lagna). Working that out is real astronomy, and this page does it precisely. It also tells you when a placement sits so close to a border that a few minutes of birth time could change it, which many apps never mention.

What this is, and what it is not. The positions here are astronomy: they match NASA’s own ephemeris to within seconds of arc for the Moon. The chart, panchang and dashas follow the traditional rules exactly. But no study has shown that these placements shape a person’s character or future: when astrology has been tested under controlled conditions, it has done no better than chance. Treat the meanings as tradition and culture, not forecast. And if you want to know yourself, try the Know Yourself questionnaire and the reading test.

What the page leaves out

The Moon uses the lunar theory in Jean Meeus’s Astronomical Algorithms (chapter 47, from ELP-2000/82) with nutation. The Sun and planets use JPL’s approximate orbital elements, good to a few hundredths of a degree between 1800 and 2050; outside those years they drift. The ayanamsa is Lahiri’s, 23°51′11″ at the start of 2000, moving 50.29″ a year; other ayanamsas differ by up to a degree or so. City coordinates are approximate; for an exact lagna, choose “my own coordinates”. Local time uses your browser’s time-zone history, which for India before 1947 may differ from records of the day. The weekday follows the traditional day, from one sunrise to the next. The page works out positions, panchang and periods only: it makes no predictions.

Quiz: five questions

Answer each one by using the lab above, then check. Good for a class: pairs work through them, the teacher reveals the answers.

  1. What is a lagna, astronomically?

    Use the lab: Read the introduction, then The real sky that night after making any kundli.

    Show answer

    The rashi that was rising on the eastern horizon at the moment and place of birth. It changes about every two hours, which is why the birth time and place matter so much.

  2. Why can two people born on the same day in different cities have different lagnas but the same Moon sign?

    Use the lab: Make a kundli, then change only the Place of birth and compare the lagna and Moon sign.

    Show answer

    The Moon’s position in the sky is the same for everyone at one moment, but which part of the sky is rising depends on where you stand on the turning Earth.

  3. HistoryHow wide is one nakshatra, and how many are there?

    Use the lab: Read the positions table and the sources.

    Show answer

    27 nakshatras, each 13°20′ of the sky (360° ÷ 27).

  4. What does the page check its Sun calculation against?

    Use the lab: Read How far to trust it.

    Show answer

    The dates of Makar Sankranti (14 January) and Mesha Sankranti (13 or 14 April), when the Sun enters sidereal Makara and Mesha.

  5. Why does the page warn when a placement is close to a border?

    Use the lab: Make a kundli and read the line under the four key cards.

    Show answer

    Birth times are often rounded to 5 or 10 minutes. If the lagna or a graha is within a few minutes of changing rashi or nakshatra, the recorded time cannot settle it.
